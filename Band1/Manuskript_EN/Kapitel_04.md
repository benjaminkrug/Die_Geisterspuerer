# Chapter 4: Condensation

Nora stood in the kitchen and stared at the window.

Behind her, the cereal bowl lay in pieces on the floor. Milk spread across the floorboards. Nora didn’t move.

The window was fogged up. A gray haze covered the glass, from the bottom up. And in the middle of the fog, at eye level, was a word. As if written with a finger. The letters were clear, the edges a little blurred.

HELP

Pressure spread beneath her ribs. Her hands were cold. Her fingers closed around the edge of the counter, so tight her knuckles turned white.

Mom was already gone. The note on the table said: “Back around six. Pizza in the fridge. Love you.” Underneath, in smaller writing: “P.S. Were you cold last night too? I had to get out the wool blanket in the middle of summer. Weird house.”

Nora stared at the last line. Mom had felt it too. The cold. Mom, who was never cold. Mom, who slept with the window open in winter.

“Nora? What’s—”

Theo shuffled into the kitchen barefoot. He stopped. His eyes went to the window.

Silence.

“NORA!”

He backed up two steps. His eyes were huge.

“I TOLD YOU!” he shouted. “I told you something was wrong! The knocking! The door! And now THIS!”

“Theo—”

“Somebody’s writing on our window! SOMEBODY WHO ISN’T THERE!”

Nora let go of the counter. Her hands were shaking, but she ignored them. She went to the window, raised her arm, and wiped her sleeve across the glass.

Gone.

Just a damp streak on her sweater.

“What are you doing?” Theo shouted. “That was evidence!”

“That was condensation,” Nora said. Her voice sounded calmer than she felt. “The windows are old. It cools down at night, and in the morning there’s moisture. And patterns in condensation—”

“That wasn’t a pattern! That was a word! A real word! Condensation doesn’t spell!”

Nora swallowed. He was right. Of course he was right. And for a moment—just one short moment—she believed it. All of it. The cold. The knocking. The word on the glass. Something was in this house. Something she couldn’t explain.

Her head spun. Her knees buckled, and she grabbed for the counter.

“We shouldn’t have gone up there,” she whispered. “Yesterday. Into the apartment. We should have—”

“Hey.” Suddenly Theo was standing next to her. His voice was calm. Strangely calm. “Nora. Breathe.”

She looked at him. Theo. Her little brother, who jumped at every little noise. He looked at her without blinking.

“Maybe Mom,” she said. Her voice shook. “Maybe this morning she—”

“Mom doesn’t write cries for help on the kitchen window, Nora. Mom writes ‘Love you’ on notes.” He put his hand on her arm for a second. “But we’ll figure it out. Okay?”

Shadow lay in the new dog bed next to the radiator. Mom had gotten it the night before, along with a leash. His head was up, and he was looking at the window. Not at Nora. Not at Theo. At the window. His ears stood straight up. His fur lay flat. Nothing like last night. No whining, no hackles.

Nora watched him. The dog was calm. Almost too calm. As if he wasn’t surprised. As if he had expected the word.

Whatever had written it, it was gone now. But it had been there. And it would come back. Nora knew it. She knew it in the part of her that didn’t listen to logic.

“We’re going to school,” Nora said.

“To school? NOW?”

“First day at the new school. We can’t miss it.”

“Yes. We can. I’m sick. I have ghost flu.”

“There’s no such thing as ghost flu.”

“There is now.”

Nora grabbed her backpack. “Get dressed. We’re leaving in twenty minutes.”

Theo crossed his arms. “You’re acting like nothing happened.”

“I’m acting like we have to go to school. Because we have to go to school.” She picked up the pieces of the cereal bowl and threw them in the trash. Wiped up the milk with a towel. Order. Control. One thing at a time.

Theo stared at her. Then he turned around and shuffled to his room. At the door, he stopped.

“In case we come home this afternoon and the house eats us,” he said, “I want it on the record. Theo was right. That’s going on my tombstone.”

“Nobody’s dying, Theo.”

“Tell that to the window.”

---

The school was big and loud and smelled like disinfectant and chalk. Hundreds of kids. Nobody knew them. A teacher explaining fractions.

Normal. This was normal.

But the word was in her head.

HELP

In math class. Between the numbers on the board.

HELP

On the way to the bathroom. In the mirror over the sink, for a split second, she saw something behind her. A movement. A dark shape. She spun around. Nothing. Empty stalls.

The goosebumps didn’t go away, though.

At recess she found Theo on a bench. He was chewing on an apple.

“Well?” she said.

“My class is okay,” he said. “One kid has a hamster in his jacket pocket. That’s solid.”

Then he went quiet. He bit into the apple. Chewed. Swallowed.

“You know what bugs me the most?” he said quietly. “The word. HELP. That’s not a threat. That’s not ‘Get out.’ Somebody’s asking for help. And we wiped the message away and went to school.”

Nora’s mouth went dry. Her hands lay in her lap, her fingers knotted together.

She didn’t have an answer for that.

---

The walk home took twelve minutes. They walked side by side through streets they didn’t know yet. Neither of them said anything.

The closer they got to Kirchgasse, the slower they walked. Without even noticing.

Number 14, with its gray walls and narrow windows. From the outside, everything looked normal. But Nora’s palms started to sweat.

Nora unlocked the front door. Then the stairwell, the smell of floor wax, the familiar creak of the stairs.

They went up to the second floor and opened the apartment door.

Shadow came down the hallway to meet them. His tail was wagging. He nudged Theo’s hand, then Nora’s. His fur lay flat. His eyes were calm.

“Hey, Shadow.” Theo scratched him behind the ears. “Everything okay here?”

Shadow licked Theo’s fingers. Everything normal. Everything fine.

Nora let out her breath. For the first time since that morning.

She went into the kitchen to get a glass of water. Put her backpack on the chair. Took off her shoes. Reached for a glass.

Then she saw the window.

The window had fogged over again. A milky layer covered the glass, from the bottom halfway up. In the middle of the afternoon. Even though the sun was shining through it.

And in the fog there were new letters. Clear. Sharp. As if written with a child’s finger.

HERE

Next to it, an arrow. Not a letter. A line with a point. The arrow pointed up.

Toward the ceiling.

Toward the third floor.

Toward Mrs. Silber’s apartment.

Nora set the glass down. Her fingers wouldn’t close right. The glass clinked against the counter. Her breath caught. Her knees went weak.

“Theo,” she said. Her voice was rough.

He came into the kitchen. Saw the window. His face went white.

Shadow squeezed between them. He went to the window, stood up on his hind legs, and nudged the glass with his nose. Right under the arrow.

This time Theo didn’t yell. He stared at the dog, then at the word. No joke. No sarcasm. Just wide eyes and lips pressed tight.

“That’s not a coincidence,” he said quietly.

Nora shook her head. “No.”

“Somebody wants us to go up there.”

“Yeah.”

“Somebody who writes words in condensation. On windows that shouldn’t be fogged up.”

Nora’s throat was dry. “Yeah.”

Silence.

The ticking of the radiator.

Then a sound from the hallway. Soft. Claws on wood.

Nora turned around.

Shadow stood at the end of the hallway. In front of the apartment door. But he wasn’t looking at the door. He was looking to the left. Toward the stairwell. Up.

Nora opened the apartment door. Shadow went out without looking back.

He sat at the foot of the stairs to the third floor. Upright. Ears forward. Motionless.

He was waiting for them.

Patient. Calm. As if he knew exactly where they had to go.
