# Chapter 1: New City, Old House

The moving truck was parked crooked in front of the building. Nora climbed out and stretched her back. Three hours on the road. Her legs felt like noodles.

Kirchgasse 14. The street was narrow and quiet. Cobblestones, houses that leaned against each other like tired old men. Nobody in sight.

Their new building had three floors, gray walls, and narrow windows with peeling paint. Cracks ran through the plaster like wrinkles on an old face.

“That looks like a horror movie,” Theo said beside her.

He was hugging his backpack to his chest like a shield.

“Oh, come on,” Nora said. “It’s an old building. They all look like that.”

“Old building means old. Old means creepy. That’s just logic, Nora.”

“That makes no sense.”

Mom came around the truck. She was smiling, but it was the smile she had been wearing for weeks. Too wide. Too forced. New job, new city, new life. She was trying to make everything look easy.

“So what do you think?”

“I say we turn around,” Theo said.

“Theo.”

“Just a suggestion.”

Nora said nothing. She looked up at the building, trying to find something to like. The entrance had a stone arch. That was pretty. Kind of.

“Come on,” Mom said, lifting a box off the truck. “Second floor. You’ll like it.”

Theo snorted.

The stairwell smelled like old wood and something sickly sweet. Floor wax, maybe. The stairs creaked under their feet. Every single one.

A mirror hung on the wall, with a crack straight across the glass. Nora saw herself in it. Her face, split in half by the crack.

“Even the mirror’s broken,” Theo muttered behind her.

“It’s antique,” Mom said.

“Antique and broken.”

The apartment door was brown and heavy and squeaked when it opened. Behind it was their new home. Big. Empty. High ceilings, a long hallway, creaky floorboards.

Nora led the way. The kitchen had a window that looked out on the courtyard, where a single tree stood, bare and crooked.

“Look.” Theo pointed into the courtyard. “Even the tree looks depressed.”

“That’s a normal tree.”

“It’s July, Nora. Normal trees have leaves.”

She kept going. Two bedrooms side by side, freshly painted. The paint smell didn’t cover what was underneath. Moldy. Damp.

At the end of the hallway was the living room. Big, with a bay window and a radiator that ticked away to itself. The wallpaper had a pattern of tiny flowers. In one spot it was torn off, and another layer of wallpaper showed underneath. Even older. Even darker. And under that, the plaster was uneven. Like an imprint. Five dents.

Theo tapped it. “That looks like a hand.”

“That’s old plaster.” Nora ran her finger over the dents. “Somebody probably leaned something against the wall once.”

“Hand-shaped old plaster. Sure.”

“Well?” Mom called from the hallway. “Space! High ceilings! Charm!”

“Charm is another word for old,” Theo said. He rubbed his arms. “Plus, it’s cold in here.”

Nora felt it too. A cold that didn’t belong in summer. It crept across the floor like something alive.

“The old windows,” she said. “Not properly insulated.”

She could hear how thin her own voice sounded. Like a line she had learned by heart without believing it.

“Or the house hates us.”

“Houses don’t hate anybody, Theo.”

“How would you know? Have you ever asked one?”

Mom clapped her hands. “Okay. Sorry, guys, I have to go in a minute.”

Nora turned around. “Already? We just got here.”

“I’ve got that meeting at the hospital. It’s my first time meeting the team—I can’t move it.” Mom smoothed Nora’s hair. “Two hours, tops. Start unpacking, pick out your rooms. Nora—”

“I’ll watch him.”

“I don’t need a babysitter,” Theo said.

“You got lost in the grocery store last week,” Nora said.

“It was a big grocery store.”

Mom laughed. For real this time. Then she picked up her bag, kissed them both on the head, and was gone.

The door thudded shut. The sound echoed through the empty hallway.

Silence.

Only the ticking of the radiator. The dripping of the faucet in the bathroom. And somewhere above them, a soft creaking, as if someone were walking slowly across floorboards.

Goosebumps crept over Nora’s arms. There was a metallic taste on her tongue.

“Did you hear that?” Theo whispered.

Nora looked up at the ceiling. Above them was the third floor. The empty apartment.

“That’s just the wood,” she said. “It swells and shrinks. Old houses make noises like that.”

“It sounded like somebody walking.”

“It didn’t.”

But it had.

“Great,” Theo said. He was sitting on a moving box, arms crossed. “First day. Strange city. Creepy apartment. And Mom’s gone.”

“Stop whining and help me unpack.”

Nora opened a box. Books. She stacked them on the floor, one after another. Sorted by size. Order helped. Order always helped. In the old apartment, she had known every sound. The hum of the fridge. The traffic outside. Here, nothing was familiar.

Theo shuffled through the apartment. Nora heard him opening and closing doors. Turning on the faucet in the kitchen. A thud. A muttered “Ow.”

“Nora?”

“What?”

“I’m taking the left room. The right one creaks louder.”

“Fine with me.”

Silence.

“Nora?”

“What now?”

“Come look out the window.”

No joke in his voice. No complaining. Theo sounded surprised.

Nora went into the living room. Theo stood at the bay window, his forehead pressed against the glass. He pointed down.

“There’s a dog down there.”

Nora stood next to him and looked down. A dog was sitting on the stone step outside the front door. Medium-sized. Dark fur, tangled and dull. Thin—even from up here she could almost see his ribs. He sat completely still and stared at the front door. Not at the street. At the door.

“How long has he been sitting there?” she murmured.

“No idea. I looked, and he was just sitting there.”

“A stray.”

“He’s waiting for someone. Look at the way he’s sitting.”

Nora wanted to say something sensible. That dogs didn’t wait for strangers. That he probably smelled food scraps. But something about him made her stop. The dog sat so still. So patient.

They went downstairs. Their footsteps echoed in the stairwell. Nora opened the front door and stepped out onto the stone step.

The dog lifted his head. He didn’t back away. He didn’t bark. He looked at her as if he had been waiting for exactly this moment. Then he turned his head. Slowly. He looked past Nora into the stairwell. As if he expected someone behind her.

Nora turned around. Nobody.

Amber eyes. Calm. Alert. Much too alert for a stray.

Nora’s mouth went dry. Not from fear. From something she couldn’t name.

“Hey,” Theo said softly. He crouched down and held out his hand. Slowly, with his fingers pointing down.

The dog sniffed it for a moment. Then he rested his chin in Theo’s hand.

“Nora.” Theo’s voice was barely more than a whisper. “He trusts me.”

“He’s hungry.”

“Can you just not explain everything for once?”

Nora went upstairs and got a bowl of water and a piece of cheese from the box of snacks. When she came back, Theo was sitting on the step. The dog had laid his head on Theo’s knee. His tail swished slowly across the stone. Once. Twice.

She set the bowl down. The dog drank. And drank. Greedy gulps.

“See?” Theo said. “Hasn’t eaten in days.”

“Don’t exaggerate.”

But she said it more quietly than usual. You could count every rib under the dull fur, tangled on his sides, but his eyes were clear. Watchful. As if he understood every word.

The dog ate the cheese in one bite. Then he lifted his head. Looked at Nora. Looked at Theo. Looked at the front door.

And stood up.

He trotted through the open door into the building. Like it was the most natural thing in the world. Like he had done it a thousand times. His claws clicked softly on the tiles.

Nora and Theo looked at each other.

“Did he just—” Theo started.

“Yeah.”

“Just like that?”

“Yeah.”

They followed him. What else were they supposed to do?

The dog went up the stairs. Calm, steady, without hesitating. He didn’t sniff at the steps, didn’t stop anywhere. He walked past their apartment door on the second floor without turning his head.

Theo looked at Nora. She shook her head. Don’t talk. Keep going.

Third floor.

Nora’s stomach tightened. It was only a dog on the stairs. That’s what she told herself. Only a dog. But the way he walked. With purpose. Without sniffing, without stopping. As if he knew exactly where he had to go.

The dog stopped in front of a door. The one on the right. The paint was peeling, and there was fine dust in the doorway, as if nobody had cleaned there in months.

Nora stepped closer. A nameplate hung next to the frame. Yellowed. The letters were handwritten, scrawly and faded.

SILBER

The dog sat down.

His hackles rose. Slowly, like a wave rolling down his back. His ears turned forward. His whole body went stiff.

The hair on the back of Nora’s neck stood up. Beside her, Theo grabbed her sleeve.

“Nora,” he whispered. “What’s he doing?”

Then the dog growled. Low. Deep in his throat.

A sound that skipped Nora’s ears.

And landed in her stomach.

He was growling at the door.

# Chapter 2: Shadow

The growling didn’t stop. It vibrated in Nora’s chest, low and deep.

The dog sat in front of the door with the SILBER nameplate and didn’t move. His dark hackles were up. His eyes were fixed on the door. Only on the door.

Theo grabbed Nora’s arm. His fingers were cold.

“Can we go?” he whispered. “Please?”

“Wait.” Nora put her hand on the door handle. The metal was icy. Much colder than it should have been. In July.

“Nora, no.”

She pressed down on the handle.

The door opened.

Dust. Stale air. Silence.

Nora waited. Three seconds. Five. Nothing happened. No sound. No draft. Only the still, heavy silence of an apartment nobody had set foot in for months.

She stepped through the doorway. The floorboards creaked under her shoes.

The hallway was long and narrow. Wallpaper, once yellow, now gray and stained. A finger-wide crack ran down the wall on the right, from the ceiling to the floor. On the left, furniture lay knocked over like toys after a tantrum. A chair on its side. A small table with one leg snapped. Next to it, a lamp with a shattered shade. Broken glass on the floor, covered with a fine layer of dust.

Nora swallowed. Her throat was dry. This didn’t look like someone had just moved out. It looked like someone had run away. In the middle of the night. Without looking back.

“Cozy,” Theo said behind her. “Very inviting. Five stars.”

“Quiet.”

“Happy to. I’m quiet. I’m the quietest person in the world. Can we go now?”

The dog had already gone ahead. Nora saw his paw prints in the dust next to her own footprints. No other tracks. Nobody had been here in months. Maybe longer.

She peeked into the kitchen. Empty shelves. A dead plant sat on the windowsill, brown and brittle as paper. Everything was still.

On the wall next to the kitchen door, she spotted scratches. Deep grooves in the plaster, as if someone had dragged heavy furniture along it. Or as if someone had dug their fingers in and held on.

Nora’s stomach clenched. But she kept going. There was a room at the end of the hallway. The door was open.

Cold.

It hit Nora like a wall. In one step, the temperature dropped like a stone. It fell twenty degrees. Maybe more. Nora’s skin tightened. Goosebumps raced over her arms, her neck, all the way down her back. Her fingers went numb. In seconds.

The air in the hallway was suddenly so cold that every breath burned in her lungs. Like winter. Like being outside when it’s below freezing. Except it was July, and the sun had been shining through the kitchen windows.

Up ahead, the dog stopped. Three steps short of the room. He sat down sideways across the hallway and blocked the way. His hackles rose from his neck all the way to his tail.

“Do you see that?” Theo whispered.

Nora saw it. The dog wouldn’t budge. He didn’t growl. He didn’t howl. He just sat there and wouldn’t let them pass. His whole body said no.

Nora leaned forward and looked past the dog into the room. Empty. No open window. No air conditioner. Nothing that could explain the cold.

Then she saw her breath. A small white cloud in front of her face.

In July. In the middle of summer.

“Okay,” Theo said. “Okay, okay, okay. We’re leaving now.”

“There’s got to be a cold pipe under the floor. Or—”

“Nora. Your breath is STEAMING. In summer. The dog won’t go in. What other explanation do you need?”

Nora bit her lip. She wanted to say something logical. Cold pipes. Bad construction. Drafts through invisible cracks.

But her heart was pounding, and her hands were shaking. And the dog sat in front of her like a wall of dark fur, with a look that said not one step farther.

“Fine,” she said. “We’ll go.”

“Smartest thing you’ve said all day.”

They didn’t walk. They ran. Theo first, then Nora, then the dog. Down the dusty hallway, over the knocked-over furniture, through the door into the stairwell.

They didn’t stop until the second floor. Nora bent over, hands on her knees. Her breath came fast and shallow. Theo leaned against the wall, both hands on his chest, as if he had to keep his heart from bursting.

“That,” he said between breaths, “was our last trip into strangers’ apartments. Forever. For all time. Until the end of the world.”

“Drama queen.”

“Survival expert.”

The dog sat on the landing and looked at them. Relaxed. Tail on the floor. As if nothing had happened.

Nora almost laughed. Almost. The dog was the calmest thing in this whole building. Calmer than she was. Calmer than Theo. Calmer than anything up there on the third floor.

---

Mom came home at six. Tired, but smiling. “Hey. Everything okay here? By the way, I ran into the neighbor downstairs, Mrs. Held. She talked like she hadn’t seen anyone in weeks.”

Nora said, “Everything’s fine.”

Theo said, “We have a dog.”

Mom set her bag down. Slowly. Her eyes went from Nora to Theo and then to the dog, who was sitting in the middle of the hallway, sweeping his tail across the floor. His tangled fur stuck out in every direction.

“What is that?”

“A dog,” Theo said.

“It looks like a shaggy rug with legs.”

“Mom, please.”

“Theo, we just moved in.”

“He doesn’t have a home.” Theo crouched down and put his arm around the dog. “He was outside the door. All alone. Skinny. And he’s super sweet.”

Mom rubbed her forehead. “Does he have a collar?”

“No,” Nora said.

“Fleas?”

Theo shrugged. “Maybe a few.”

“Theo.”

The dog stood up, trotted over to Mom, and rested his head against her knee. He looked up at Mom. Calm. Patient.

Mom sighed. “For now.”

Theo pumped his fist. “Yes!”

“For now, Theo. If someone claims him—”

“Nobody’s going to claim him. He belongs with us.”

Nora leaned against the doorframe and watched the dog. He had lain down next to Theo. His tail swished slowly across the floor. One. Two. One. Two.

Something about this dog pulled at her. He was a stray they knew nothing about. Dirty, skinny, full of fleas. Still, she had the feeling he belonged here. With them. And that scared her almost more than the cold upstairs.

“What should we call him?” Mom said.

Theo tilted his head. He looked at the dog. The dark fur, the bright eyes, the way he moved. Softly. Without a sound.

“Shadow,” Theo said.

The dog lifted his head. His ears twitched. A quick flick, as if he had recognized the name.

Nora nodded slowly. The name fit. Perfectly.

---

They ate pizza between moving boxes. Mom told them about her first day at the hospital. Nora nodded in the right places. But her mind was upstairs. On the third floor. In the room that was so cold she had seen her breath.

After the third slice, Mom’s eyelids started to droop.

Theo fed Shadow half a slice of pepperoni. The dog swallowed it in one bite.

“Tomorrow we’re buying dog food,” Nora said.

“And a leash. And a dog bed. And treats.”

“We don’t have money for treats.”

“Shadow needs treats. It’s scientifically proven.”

“Says who?”

“Me. Just now.”

Nora shook her head. But she smiled. A little. For the first time since they got here, it almost felt like home. Shadow lay on the floor between them, his head on his paws.

Mom fell asleep on the couch. Within seconds. It had been like that since the move. She worked until she dropped, and then she slept so deeply you could vacuum right next to her.

Nora stayed where she was and looked out the window. Kirchgasse was quiet in the evening light. Warm air drifted in.

Warm. Warm everywhere. Except upstairs. Except in that one room, the one Shadow had refused to go into.

Out of the corner of her eye, she saw something. A flicker on the wall, like a dark shape moving without any light to cast it. She turned her head. Nothing. Just the empty wall in the evening light.

---

Night. Silence. Nora lay in bed and stared at the ceiling. Next to the bed, a moving box served as her nightstand. Her alarm clock sat on top. 11:47.

She rolled onto her side. Closed her eyes. Tried to think about something normal. School. Books. The park she had seen on the drive here.

Shadow lay outside her door. She could hear him breathing. Even. Calm.

Nora squeezed her eyes shut tighter.

Then the breathing stopped.

No more snuffling. No rustling. Only a soft click of claws as Shadow got up. He was standing now. Motionless. Focused on something Nora couldn’t see.

She opened her eyes.

Then she heard it.

Thump. Thump. Thump.

Pause.

Thump. Thump. Thump.

Nora held her breath. Her heart was beating so loud she could hear it in her ears. The knocking came from the wall. Right behind her headboard. Directly above her.

Straight from Mrs. Silber’s apartment.

Thump. Thump. Thump.

Three times. Pause. Three times. Always the same. Always the same rhythm.

Nora lay frozen under the covers. Her fingers dug into the fabric.

Outside the door, Shadow still stood motionless. Not a sound. Not a breath.

Then, very softly, a low whine.

# Chapter 3: Three Times

Thump. Thump. Thump.

Nora lay frozen under the covers. Her heart hammered against her ribs. The knocking came from the wall behind her headboard. Three times. Pause. Three times. Pause.

The apartment was dark. Moonlight fell through the bare windows and painted pale rectangles on the floor. The moving boxes stood against the wall like silent figures.

Nora counted the seconds between the knocks. Exactly four. Always four. Pipes didn’t keep a beat.

Nora pushed back the covers and got up. The wooden floor creaked under her feet. Shadow was sitting in the hallway. Upright, ears forward, hackles up. He didn’t growl. He waited.

That was worse than growling. It was as if he had heard this before.

Nora crept to Theo’s room. She pushed the handle down and eased the door open. Theo lay curled up under his blanket. Only a tuft of hair stuck out.

“Theo,” Nora whispered.

The blanket twitched.

“Theo, wake up.”

“Awake,” he muttered. “Been awake since the first knock. I’m just practicing being a blanket.”

Nora sat down on the edge of his bed. “Do you hear that?”

Thump. Thump. Thump.

Theo pulled the blanket down. His eyes were wide. “Of course I hear it. They can probably hear it on the moon.”

“It’s coming from the wall,” Nora said. “From upstairs. From Mrs. Silber’s apartment.”

Thump. Thump. Thump. The wall vibrated under her hand. Nora’s ears filled with pressure, as if she had ridden an elevator up too fast.

“Perfect,” Theo said. “The empty, creepy apartment with the ice room. Of course.”

Nora crossed her arms. “It’s the pipes. The building is old. Pipes swell and shrink.”

“To a beat?” Theo said. “Three times, pause, three times, pause? Those are the most organized pipes in the world.”

“There’s an explanation,” Nora said.

But what if Theo was right? The thought was there, short and sharp as a pinprick. She pushed it away. Right away. But it had been there.

“Yeah. Ghosts. Definitely ghosts.” Theo swallowed. “Can we pull the blanket over our heads now and pretend nothing’s happening?”

“I’m going up to check.”

Theo sat bolt upright. “Please tell me you’re not serious.”

“If it’s the pipes, we’ll see. If not—”

“If not, we’re dead. That’s how every horror movie starts.”

“This isn’t a movie, Theo.”

“No. You can turn off a movie.”

“Stay here. Watch Shadow.”

“Watch Shadow?” Theo stared at her. “Shadow weighs sixty-five pounds. If anything, he’s babysitting me.”

Nora stood up. Something cold stabbed through her chest. But she would never admit that her hands were shaking. Not in front of Theo.

Shadow was still waiting in the hallway. He turned his head toward Nora. His eyes were calm. Alert. His hackles were still up.

“It’s okay, Shadow,” Nora whispered.

The dog didn’t move. He just looked at her. As if he wanted to tell her no, nothing was okay at all.

Nora took the flashlight off the shelf. Her fingers closed tight around the handle.

Shadow stood up. He took one step toward the front door. Then he stopped and planted himself in front of Theo’s room. Between Theo and the stairwell.

She opened the apartment door and stepped into the stairwell.

Cold.

Instantly. Like a wall of ice pressing against her. In July. In the middle of the night. The air tasted like metal and old stone.

Nora’s breath made a white cloud in front of her face. She clenched her free hand into a fist. Her fingers were numb with cold.

The stairs lay in darkness. The flashlight threw a trembling circle on the wall. Outside the circle, the darkness was deep and black.

Nora climbed the stairs. Every single one creaked under her weight. The walls of the stairwell were gray in the flashlight beam. Somewhere, water was dripping. Or something that sounded like water.

The knocking grew louder. It came from above, from behind the door to Mrs. Silber’s apartment.

Thump. Thump. Thump.

Pause.

Thump. Thump. Thump.

Nora reached the landing in front of the apartment. She pointed the flashlight at the door.

Her heart skipped a beat.

The door was closed. Shut tight. They had left it open that afternoon. Theo had run out first, Nora right behind him. Nobody had pulled the door shut.

A prickle ran up her back, over her neck, into her scalp. Nora reached out. Her fingers were shaking. They touched the wood.

Cold. Ice-cold. Like the room Shadow wouldn’t go into.

And at that exact moment, the knocking stopped.

Silence. Total silence. No creaking, no wind, nothing. Only Nora’s breath, drifting in small clouds through the flashlight beam.

Nora stood in front of the closed door and didn’t move. Ten seconds. Twenty. Thirty. The silence pressed on her ears.

She put her ear to the cold wood. Behind it: nothing. No rustling. No breathing. No knocking. Only empty, icy silence.

She turned around and went down the stairs. Fast. Don’t run. Whatever happens, don’t run. Nora pressed her lips together. Every step controlled. Every step steady.

Her knees had other ideas. They almost gave out.

But she didn’t run. She just walked fast. Very fast.

Back in the apartment, Theo was waiting in the hallway. Shadow sat next to him. Theo had one hand buried in the fur on Shadow’s neck.

“Well?” he whispered. “Please tell me you told it to move out.”

Nora put the flashlight down. “The door’s closed.”

Theo blinked. “We left it open.”

“I know.”

“Nora. We left it open. Nobody lives up there.” He shifted from one foot to the other. “So somebody closed it. Or something.”

“Maybe a draft,” Nora said.

“A draft. In a stairwell with no windows. In summer. That slams a heavy wooden door.”

Nora bit her lip. The cold from the stairwell was still in her bones.

“There’s an explanation,” she said.

“That’s the third time you’ve said that,” Theo muttered. “And every time it sounds less convincing.”

Shadow tilted his head. His eyes went from Nora to Theo and back. Then he got up, trotted to his spot between the bedrooms, and lay down. A deep sigh. As if the show was over for tonight.

“See?” Theo said. “Even the dog thinks it’s suspicious.”

“The dog is going to sleep. That’s the opposite of suspicious.”

“He’s processing. Dogs process creepy stuff in their sleep. I bet it says so somewhere.”

Nora shook her head. But a smile tugged at the corners of her mouth. Theo was annoying. But he was the only one who could almost make her laugh in the middle of the night.

“We’ll talk about it tomorrow,” she said. “In daylight. When everything looks normal.”

“Nothing looks normal here, Nora. We live in a house that knocks.”

“Tomorrow.”

“Great. Because everybody knows ghosts keep business hours.”

Nora went back to her room. She lay down, the blanket pulled up to her chin, and stared at the ceiling.

The door had been closed. They had left it open, and now it was closed. Someone—or something—had shut it. In an empty apartment where nobody had lived for months.

The knocking didn’t come back. But sleep didn’t either. Every creak sounded like footsteps. Every gust of wind like fingers on glass.

Sometime just before five, as the first gray light seeped through the windows, she fell asleep.

---

Sunlight came through the window. Nora stood in the kitchen and poured cereal into a bowl. Her eyes burned. Her head felt heavy.

Theo sat at the table, his chin propped on his hands. Dark circles under his eyes. He looked like someone who had slept for three hours. Because he had slept for three hours.

“Mom?” Nora said.

“Already gone. Early shift.” Theo stirred his chocolate milk halfheartedly. “Left a note.”

Shadow lay under the table, chewing on a toy bone. Relaxed. Normal. As if nothing had happened last night.

“Did you sleep?” Nora said.

“If you want to call it that.” Theo rubbed his eyes. “I stared at the wall all night. In case it knocked again.”

“Did it?”

“No. Which is almost creepier.” He took a sip of chocolate milk. “Silence after knocking is worse than knocking. That’s my new life lesson.”

Shadow lifted his head under the table. He looked up at the ceiling. Just for a second. Then he went back to chewing.

Nora went to the window. She wanted to open it. Fresh air.

Then she stopped.

The window was fogged up. A gray film covered the glass, damp and cloudy. In the middle of July. In the middle of the morning, with the sun out. Cold air brushed over Nora’s hands.

Something had been drawn in the fog. Lines. Marks. At first she thought it was random, just drops trickling down. Then she stepped closer.

They weren’t drops. They were letters. At eye level, written with a finger, clear and sharp. Impossible to miss.

HELP

Nora’s cereal bowl crashed to the floor.

# Chapter 4: Condensation

Nora stood in the kitchen and stared at the window.

Behind her, the cereal bowl lay in pieces on the floor. Milk spread across the floorboards. Nora didn’t move.

The window was fogged up. A gray haze covered the glass, from the bottom up. And in the middle of the fog, at eye level, was a word. As if written with a finger. The letters were clear, the edges a little blurred.

HELP

Pressure spread beneath her ribs. Her hands were cold. Her fingers closed around the edge of the counter, so tight her knuckles turned white.

Mom was already gone. The note on the table said: “Back around six. Pizza in the fridge. Love you.” Underneath, in smaller writing: “P.S. Were you cold last night too? I had to get out the wool blanket in the middle of summer. Weird house.”

Nora stared at the last line. Mom had felt it too. The cold. Mom, who was never cold. Mom, who slept with the window open in winter.

“Nora? What’s—”

Theo shuffled into the kitchen barefoot. He stopped. His eyes went to the window.

Silence.

“NORA!”

He backed up two steps. His eyes were huge.

“I TOLD YOU!” he shouted. “I told you something was wrong! The knocking! The door! And now THIS!”

“Theo—”

“Somebody’s writing on our window! SOMEBODY WHO ISN’T THERE!”

Nora let go of the counter. Her hands were shaking, but she ignored them. She went to the window, raised her arm, and wiped her sleeve across the glass.

Gone.

Just a damp streak on her sweater.

“What are you doing?” Theo shouted. “That was evidence!”

“That was condensation,” Nora said. Her voice sounded calmer than she felt. “The windows are old. It cools down at night, and in the morning there’s moisture. And patterns in condensation—”

“That wasn’t a pattern! That was a word! A real word! Condensation doesn’t spell!”

Nora swallowed. He was right. Of course he was right. And for a moment—just one short moment—she believed it. All of it. The cold. The knocking. The word on the glass. Something was in this house. Something she couldn’t explain.

Her head spun. Her knees buckled, and she grabbed for the counter.

“We shouldn’t have gone up there,” she whispered. “Yesterday. Into the apartment. We should have—”

“Hey.” Suddenly Theo was standing next to her. His voice was calm. Strangely calm. “Nora. Breathe.”

She looked at him. Theo. Her little brother, who jumped at every little noise. He looked at her without blinking.

“Maybe Mom,” she said. Her voice shook. “Maybe this morning she—”

“Mom doesn’t write cries for help on the kitchen window, Nora. Mom writes ‘Love you’ on notes.” He put his hand on her arm for a second. “But we’ll figure it out. Okay?”

Shadow lay in the new dog bed next to the radiator. Mom had gotten it the night before, along with a leash. His head was up, and he was looking at the window. Not at Nora. Not at Theo. At the window. His ears stood straight up. His fur lay flat. Nothing like last night. No whining, no hackles.

Nora watched him. The dog was calm. Almost too calm. As if he wasn’t surprised. As if he had expected the word.

Whatever had written it, it was gone now. But it had been there. And it would come back. Nora knew it. She knew it in the part of her that didn’t listen to logic.

“We’re going to school,” Nora said.

“To school? NOW?”

“First day at the new school. We can’t miss it.”

“Yes. We can. I’m sick. I have ghost flu.”

“There’s no such thing as ghost flu.”

“There is now.”

Nora grabbed her backpack. “Get dressed. We’re leaving in twenty minutes.”

Theo crossed his arms. “You’re acting like nothing happened.”

“I’m acting like we have to go to school. Because we have to go to school.” She picked up the pieces of the cereal bowl and threw them in the trash. Wiped up the milk with a towel. Order. Control. One thing at a time.

Theo stared at her. Then he turned around and shuffled to his room. At the door, he stopped.

“In case we come home this afternoon and the house eats us,” he said, “I want it on the record. Theo was right. That’s going on my tombstone.”

“Nobody’s dying, Theo.”

“Tell that to the window.”

---

The school was big and loud and smelled like disinfectant and chalk. Hundreds of kids. Nobody knew them. A teacher explaining fractions.

Normal. This was normal.

But the word was in her head.

HELP

In math class. Between the numbers on the board.

HELP

On the way to the bathroom. In the mirror over the sink, for a split second, she saw something behind her. A movement. A dark shape. She spun around. Nothing. Empty stalls.

The goosebumps didn’t go away, though.

At recess she found Theo on a bench. He was chewing on an apple.

“Well?” she said.

“My class is okay,” he said. “One kid has a hamster in his jacket pocket. That’s solid.”

Then he went quiet. He bit into the apple. Chewed. Swallowed.

“You know what bugs me the most?” he said quietly. “The word. HELP. That’s not a threat. That’s not ‘Get out.’ Somebody’s asking for help. And we wiped the message away and went to school.”

Nora’s mouth went dry. Her hands lay in her lap, her fingers knotted together.

She didn’t have an answer for that.

---

The walk home took twelve minutes. They walked side by side through streets they didn’t know yet. Neither of them said anything.

The closer they got to Kirchgasse, the slower they walked. Without even noticing.

Number 14, with its gray walls and narrow windows. From the outside, everything looked normal. But Nora’s palms started to sweat.

Nora unlocked the front door. Then the stairwell, the smell of floor wax, the familiar creak of the stairs.

They went up to the second floor and opened the apartment door.

Shadow came down the hallway to meet them. His tail was wagging. He nudged Theo’s hand, then Nora’s. His fur lay flat. His eyes were calm.

“Hey, Shadow.” Theo scratched him behind the ears. “Everything okay here?”

Shadow licked Theo’s fingers. Everything normal. Everything fine.

Nora let out her breath. For the first time since that morning.

She went into the kitchen to get a glass of water. Put her backpack on the chair. Took off her shoes. Reached for a glass.

Then she saw the window.

The window had fogged over again. A milky layer covered the glass, from the bottom halfway up. In the middle of the afternoon. Even though the sun was shining through it.

And in the fog there were new letters. Clear. Sharp. As if written with a child’s finger.

HERE

Next to it, an arrow. Not a letter. A line with a point. The arrow pointed up.

Toward the ceiling.

Toward the third floor.

Toward Mrs. Silber’s apartment.

Nora set the glass down. Her fingers wouldn’t close right. The glass clinked against the counter. Her breath caught. Her knees went weak.

“Theo,” she said. Her voice was rough.

He came into the kitchen. Saw the window. His face went white.

Shadow squeezed between them. He went to the window, stood up on his hind legs, and nudged the glass with his nose. Right under the arrow.

This time Theo didn’t yell. He stared at the dog, then at the word. No joke. No sarcasm. Just wide eyes and lips pressed tight.

“That’s not a coincidence,” he said quietly.

Nora shook her head. “No.”

“Somebody wants us to go up there.”

“Yeah.”

“Somebody who writes words in condensation. On windows that shouldn’t be fogged up.”

Nora’s throat was dry. “Yeah.”

Silence.

The ticking of the radiator.

Then a sound from the hallway. Soft. Claws on wood.

Nora turned around.

Shadow stood at the end of the hallway. In front of the apartment door. But he wasn’t looking at the door. He was looking to the left. Toward the stairwell. Up.

Nora opened the apartment door. Shadow went out without looking back.

He sat at the foot of the stairs to the third floor. Upright. Ears forward. Motionless.

He was waiting for them.

Patient. Calm. As if he knew exactly where they had to go.

# Chapter 5: Mrs. Silber’s Apartment

Shadow waited.

He sat at the foot of the stairs to the third floor. Upright. Motionless. His eyes on Nora. His tail lay still on the floor. No wagging. No twitching. Just that patient waiting that said: I’m ready. Are you?

Nora stood in the hallway. Her heart beat faster. Behind her, back in the kitchen, was the window. The word HERE. The arrow pointing up.

“He wants us to go up there,” Theo said quietly.

“I know.”

“To the apartment.”

“I know, Theo.”

Silence. The ticking of the radiator. Shadow’s breathing.

“Last time,” Theo said, “we ran. I want it on the record. We ran.”

“I remember.”

“Good. Just so we’ve got that down before we make the same mistake again.”

Nora took a deep breath. Her palms were damp. Her mouth was dry. Every part of her said: Don’t go up there. Stay here. Close the door.

But the word was there on the window behind her. HERE. Like a finger pointing up.

“Come on,” she said.

“That’s what I was afraid of.”

They climbed the stairs. Nora first. Shadow next to her. His claws clicked softly on the wooden steps. Theo brought up the rear. He held on to the railing. His knuckles were white.

The third floor. The landing. The door with the SILBER nameplate.

It was open. Just a crack. But this time was different.

This time the air was cool. Not icy like last time. More like a basement in summer. Nora swallowed. There was a metallic taste on her tongue. Like coins. Strange and wrong.

Nora stopped. She put her hand on the doorframe. The wood was cool, not cold. No frost under her fingertips. She couldn’t see her breath.

Shadow stood next to her. He sniffed at the doorway. Once. Twice.

Then he went in.

Nora’s heart skipped a beat. Last time, Shadow had refused. He had planted himself sideways across the hallway. His whole body had said no. And now he trotted through the doorway like it was the most natural thing in the world.

“Did he just—” Theo started.

“Yeah.”

“He’s going in. He’s just going in. The dog who growled at the door last time.”

“I can see that, Theo.”

Theo gulped. “Great. So either it’s safe now. Or the dog gave up.”

“Dogs don’t give up.”

“How would you know?”

Nora followed Shadow. The dusty floorboards creaked under her feet. Theo came after her. He stayed close behind her. So close that she could feel his breath on her neck.

The hallway was dim. On the left, the knocked-over furniture, the broken table. Everything just like the first time. Dust on every surface.

But the air was different. The heaviness from last time was gone. That feeling of something pressing down on the apartment, like a hand on her chest. Nora could breathe easier. Still, her fingers tingled.

Shadow went ahead. He didn’t go to the room at the end of the hallway. Not to the cold room. He turned left, into a room Nora had barely noticed the first time. They had been too busy running.

Nora followed him.

The room was small. Maybe ten feet by twelve. The shutters were closed. Only a thin strip of afternoon light came through a gap. Specks of dust danced in the beam.

There were books along the wall across from the window. Stacks of them, right on the floor, leaning against the wall. Old covers, some leather, some brittle cardboard.

“What is all this?” Theo whispered.

Nora stepped closer. There was a small table next to the books, crowded with burned-down candles. The wax had run over the edge of the table in thick drips and hardened on the wood.

“Okay,” Theo whispered. “That looks like a witch’s lab.”

“That looks like a study.”

“Exactly. A witch’s study.”

Nora moved in closer. Her eyes moved over the books. The titles were faded. She could read a few. “Gravenstedt: A Chronicle.” “Cemetery Register 1830–1900.” “The History of Kirchgasse.” Old books about the city. History books and old city records.

“Theo, come look—”

She turned around. Theo wasn’t there.

“Theo?”

No answer. Only dust. Only silence. The hallway outside the door was dark and empty.

Nora’s heart beat faster. “Theo!” Louder now. Her voice echoed off the walls. Shadow lifted his head and pricked up his ears.

Three seconds. Four. Five.

Then footsteps. Theo appeared in the doorway with a dusty glass in his hand. “What? I was just in the kitchen. There are still some old glasses in the—”

“Tell me when you go somewhere!” Nora’s voice was sharper than she meant it to be. Her heart was still hammering.

Theo raised his eyebrows. “I was ten feet away.”

“Doesn’t matter. Not alone. Not here.”

He set the glass down and nodded slowly. “Okay. Got it.”

Next to the stacks of books, something was hanging on the wall. Notes. Dozens of them. Pinned up with thumbtacks, in rows up and down the wall. Nora went closer and squinted.

Handwriting. Small and scrawly, in black ink. Each note had a name on it. Next to the name, a date. Some had an address. Some had a short sentence, barely readable.

“Heinrich Voss, 1847, Mühlenweg 3.”

“Who writes stuff like that down?” Theo murmured behind her.

Nora kept reading. Dozens of names. The oldest notes were yellowed, the ink almost invisible. Some of the dates went back as far as 1840.

“1840?” Theo repeated. “That’s almost two hundred years ago.”

“Yeah.”

Many of the names had a small check mark next to them. Neat, in black ink.

Only the note at the very bottom looked different. The ink was blue instead of black. The handwriting was shakier, as if someone had written it in a hurry.

*Lina Vogt? – 1974?*

No check mark. Just two question marks.

Nora scanned the rows. Next to every name, a date. Always just one. No “born,” no “died.” Only a single day. And the longer she looked, the more something came together in her head. Something she didn’t want to say out loud. A thought, cold and heavy, that settled in her stomach.

Theo stared at the wall. He shifted from one foot to the other. Then he said it.

“It’s like a list of dead people.”

Nora swallowed. Cold pressure spread through her chest. Of course. Of course that’s what it was. Not an address book. Not a list of phone numbers. These were dead people.

“But why?” Theo said. “Why would somebody write a list of dead people on the wall?”

Shadow lay in the middle of the room. On the dusty floor, his nose on his paws. His eyes followed Nora. Calm. Alert. As if he were waiting for her to understand.

Nora turned away from the forest of notes. A small dresser stood next to the table with the candles. Three drawers, all closed. More notes lay on top of it, along with a pencil and a book with its cover open.

And a photo.

Nora picked it up. Black and white. The edges yellowed, a crease in one corner. It showed a woman. Old. Maybe seventy. White hair in a braid that hung over her shoulder. A narrow face with deep wrinkles and a mouth that was almost smiling. But it was the eyes that held Nora. Alert. Clear. Looking straight into the camera.

A dog sat next to the woman. Medium-sized. Dark fur.

Amber eyes.

Nora’s fingers started to shake. She turned the photo over. On the back, in the same scrawly handwriting, it said: “M. Silber, 2019.”

She looked over at Shadow. The dog in the photo was younger. His fur thicker, his body stronger. But that look. The same calm. The way he sat next to the woman. Upright. Patient.

“Theo.”

He came over and looked over her shoulder. Then he went quiet.

“That’s him,” Theo whispered. “That’s Shadow.”

“Yeah.”

“Next to Mrs. Silber.”

“Yeah.”

“So that means Shadow was her dog.”

Nora nodded slowly. Her heart beat hard against her ribs. She looked at Shadow on the floor. The dog lifted his head and looked back at her.

It was no accident, Nora thought. Not him showing up at our door. Not him on the stairs. He had come home.

“That’s why he brought us here,” Nora said. “That’s why he waits by the stairs. This was his home.”

Theo knelt down next to Shadow and put a hand on the dog’s back. Shadow pressed his head against Theo’s knee. A soft sound. Not a growl. Not a whine. A sigh. Deep and long, as if he had been holding it in for a long time.

“Hey, buddy,” Theo murmured. “Now we know.”

Nora swallowed the lump in her throat. She put the photo back and looked around. The books. The candles. The list of names.

Mrs. Silber had done something in this apartment. Something with these names. Something with these dead people.

“What was she doing?” Nora murmured. “Why would somebody hang a list of dead people on the wall?”

Theo stood up. He wiped his hands on his pants. “Maybe she was a historian. Or one of those family-tree people.”

“With candles?”

“Historians like candles. They’re nostalgic.”

“You don’t know what nostalgic means.”

“Yes, I do. It means old and dusty. Like this place.”

Nora snorted. Almost a laugh. Theo looked at her with a lopsided grin. Even here, in an abandoned apartment full of dust and notes, he could almost make her laugh. Almost.

Suddenly Shadow lifted his head. Sharply. His hackles rose.

Nora’s smile vanished. Her skin tightened. She knew this sign. Shadow was their warning system. When his hackles rose, something was coming.

The dog stared at the door. His ears stood straight up and forward. His body was tense, every muscle visible under the dark fur.

Nora grabbed Theo’s arm. He had seen it too. His face was pale.

Then they heard it.

From the hallway. Behind them. A sound. Soft. Fast.

Footsteps. Small footsteps. A child’s feet on a wooden floor.

Running.

Nora’s pulse raced. Her legs went stiff. The footsteps came closer, pattering over the floorboards, left, right, left, fast, faster—

And stopped.

Silence.

Nora held her breath. Her fingers dug into Theo’s sleeve. Theo stood frozen next to her. His face was white. His eyes were huge.

Shadow whined. Softly. Pitifully.

Not a sound from the hallway. No creaking. No rustling. Only the heavy, pressing silence of an empty apartment.

An apartment where they were alone.

An apartment where someone had just been running.

Someone small. Someone with bare feet on old wood.

Someone who wasn’t there.

# Chapter 6: The List of Names

They ran.

Nora jumped down the stairs, two at a time. Theo was ahead of her. Shadow between them. The dog’s claws scraped over the wood.

It was warm in the stairwell. The normal kind of warm. No ice, and she couldn’t see her breath. Only the creaking of the steps and the pounding of Nora’s heart.

Front door. Theo yanked it open. Sunlight. Nora stumbled onto the sidewalk and stopped. She bent over, hands on her knees. Her breath came way too fast.

Theo stood two steps away, his hands on his head. His face was pale. Shadow sat next to him, panting. His fur lay flat. No raised hackles. As if out here was a different world.

“Okay,” Theo said. He took three deep breaths. “Okay. That happened.”

“Yeah,” Nora said.

“We just ran out of a ghost apartment.” He pointed at Shadow. “Our dog led us in there.” Pause. “This is my life now.”

Nora wiped the sweat off her forehead. Her heartbeat slowly went back to normal. But her fingers were numb, as if she had touched ice.

“There were footsteps,” she said. “In the hallway. Behind us.”

“A child’s footsteps, Nora. Little running feet. On a wooden floor where nobody was standing.”

Nora swallowed. He was right. She had heard it. Clear and sharp. Quick, light footsteps. And then silence.

“Can I say something?” Theo muttered.

“Could I stop you?”

“We had a stray outside our door. The stray led us into a stranger’s apartment. In the apartment, there’s a list of dead people. A photo of an old woman with our dog. And ghost-kid feet in the hallway.” He scratched Shadow behind the ears. “That’s not normal. That’s the opposite of normal.”

Nora straightened up. Kirchgasse was quiet in front of them. Cars were parked along the curb. Somewhere, someone laughed.

The world was normal. Just not theirs anymore.

“The list of names,” Nora said.

Theo looked at her. “What?”

“The list on the wall. The names and dates. You said it was like a list of dead people.”

“Theo said a lot of things. Theo also wanted to get out. Did anybody listen to Theo? No.”

“Listen.” Nora pressed her lips together. The fear gave way to curiosity. It always happened like that. First the pounding heart, then the questions. “Mrs. Silber lived there. The apartment is full of notes. And that list—names, dates, some from as far back as 1840. Those people aren’t alive.”

“Obviously.”

“What if Mrs. Silber had something to do with those dead people? What if she helped them?”

Theo stared at her. “Helped? With what?”

“I don’t know. Not yet. But the photo. Shadow was in the photo. Younger, but definitely him. Mrs. Silber knew our dog.”

Shadow lifted his head and looked at Nora. His eyes were calm. Attentive. As if he were listening.

“And the check marks,” Nora said. “Most of the names had one. Like a to-do list.”

Theo swallowed. “A to-do list. For dead people.”

“For people who needed help.” Nora pictured the blue note again. “Only the name at the very bottom didn’t have one. Lina Vogt. 1974. With question marks.”

“Whatever Mrs. Silber was doing,” Nora said slowly, “she couldn’t do it for Lina Vogt.”

Theo bit his lower lip. “And where is Mrs. Silber now?”

“That’s what I want to find out.”

“Of course you do.” Theo sighed. “Other kids go home and play video games. We investigate ghost cases.”

---

The city library was a twelve-minute walk away, at the edge of the Old Town. A wide building of yellow sandstone with tall windows.

Shadow had to stay outside. Theo tied the leash to a bike rack.

“Wait here,” he said. “And if anything creepy happens, bark.”

Shadow lay down and yawned.

“He doesn’t take me seriously,” Theo said.

“Welcome to my world,” Nora said.

The library smelled like old paper and coffee. Shelves up to the ceiling. Somewhere, a computer hummed. Nora liked libraries. Here, there were answers. Here, there was order.

“Smells like Grandma’s attic,” Theo muttered. “Just without the cookies.”

A woman with glasses and a gray bun sat behind the desk. Nora went over to her.

“Excuse me. We’re looking for information about someone who lived in Gravenstedt. M. Silber.”

The woman looked at them over the top of her glasses. “For school?”

“Yes,” Nora said quickly.

Theo raised his eyebrows. Nora stepped on his foot.

“The newspaper archive is in the basement,” the woman said. “The last five years are digital. Everything before that is on microfiche.”

“Thanks.”

Theo followed her down the stairs. “Now we’re lying too,” he muttered. “Ghost hunters and liars. Great résumé.”

“That wasn’t a lie. We’re learning something. That counts as school.”

“What class is that? Spookology?”

The basement was cool and quiet. Fluorescent lights hummed on the ceiling. Long rows of shelves with bound newspapers. There were three old computers against the wall.

Nora sat down at the first one. The newspaper’s search page loaded slowly. She typed: “M. Silber Gravenstedt.”

One result.

Nora’s fingers tingled. She clicked on it.

A short article. Local section, at the very bottom of the page. Between a notice about road construction and a report on a school fair. Three paragraphs. No photo.

“There,” Nora said.

Theo leaned over her shoulder. They read it together.

LOCAL WOMAN MISSING. Margret Silber, 75, who has lived on Kirchgasse for many years, has been missing since March 14. Police found no one in her apartment. “No signs of violence or a break-in,” a spokesperson said. “Mrs. Silber appears to have left the apartment of her own free will.”

That was all. Three paragraphs. No follow-up. Nora searched again. And again. Nothing.

“Three paragraphs,” Theo muttered. “Seventy-five years old and three paragraphs. My hamster report was longer.”

Nora stared at the screen. Margret Silber. Seventy-five years old. Lived alone. Kirchgasse. The apartment on the third floor.

“The police say she left of her own free will,” Nora said. “But the apartment didn’t look like she wanted to leave. Knocked-over furniture. Scratches on the walls.”

“It looked like she was running from something,” Theo said.

The fluorescent lights hummed. The back of Nora’s neck prickled. She turned around. Behind her, only empty rows of chairs and the buzz of the fluorescent tubes. She thought of Shadow, waiting up there outside the library. Calm. Patient. As if he had known what they would find.

“What scared her like that?” Nora whispered. “What could scare a woman who lived alone in a house like that for so long—” She broke off.

“What do you mean?” Theo said.

Nora shook her head. She didn’t know yet. But the puzzle pieces were right in front of her. The list of names. The notes. The photo with Shadow. An old woman who had lived in an apartment full of clues about the dead. And who had disappeared one night.

“Seventy-five,” she murmured. “Seventy-five years old, her whole life in Gravenstedt. And the only proof she ever existed is three paragraphs in the local section.”

“And an apartment full of notes,” Theo said quietly.

Nora stared at the screen a moment longer. Then she typed a new name into the search box. *Lina Vogt.*

No results.

“Of course not,” Theo said. “The lady said only the last five years are digital. It says 1974 next to Lina.”

Nora turned around. There was a machine in the corner that looked like an old computer with a glass plate and knobs. Next to it, a cabinet with hundreds of little boxes, labeled with years.

“Microfiche,” she said.

“Bless you.”

“Little strips of film with photos of newspaper pages. Like a mini archive.”

“Sounds like something somebody in a horror movie would use,” Theo muttered. “Dark basement. Old archives. Missing person. This is chapter one of *We Should Have Stayed Home*.”

Nora pulled out the box *1974: October to December*.

She put the film on the glass plate. Her fingers shook a little, but she ignored it. She turned the knob. Newspaper pages slid across the screen. Headlines from another time, ads for furniture stores, photos of the Christmas market.

“It’s like Netflix without the picture or the sound,” Theo said, and yawned.

“Focus. We need 1974.”

“I am focused. I’m just also bored. You can be both at once.”

Nora looked up. Up near the ceiling, there was a narrow window, right at sidewalk level. Shadow was sitting outside. He was whining. She couldn’t hear it, but she could see it in his body. The trembling. The flattened ears. He was staring down the street. Toward the Old Town.

She looked away and kept turning the knob. Early November.

Then something caught her eye.

A short article. Six lines. At the very bottom of page four, between an ad for Dr. Kessler, Dentist, and the weather report. Nora had almost missed it.

Her scalp prickled.

“Theo.”

He looked up. “What?”

“I found her.”

Theo pulled his chair closer. The scrape of the chair legs echoed through the quiet room.

They read it together. Nora’s heart was pounding in her throat.

The article was short. Cold. Just the facts.

“Lina Vogt, 12, daughter of Mr. and Mrs. Vogt of Kirchgasse 14, died November 3, 1974, after an accident.”

Six lines. That was all. Not a word about what kind of accident. A girl had died, and the newspaper had six lines to spare. Between the dentist and the weather.

But there was a photo. Small, grainy, barely visible on the microfiche. A class photo. Three rows of kids in front of a brick wall. Nora checked the edges of the picture. Bottom right. A girl with a dark braid. Lina Vogt. She stood between two other kids, a little crooked, as if she had moved just when the picture was taken. A completely normal girl. That was the hard part.

“Twelve,” Theo whispered. “She was twelve.”

Nora swallowed. Twelve. Just like her. A girl her age. Back when there were no cell phones and newspapers were stored on strips of film.

But that wasn’t why her hands suddenly went ice-cold. Not her age. Not the accident.

“Theo,” she said. Her voice was barely audible. “Read the address.”

He leaned forward. Read. His lips moved without a sound.

The blood drained from his face like water out of a bathtub.

“Kirchgasse 14,” he whispered.

Nora nodded. Her fingers dug into the edge of the table.

“That’s our address.”

Nora thought of Shadow outside on the sidewalk. The way he had stared down the street. Trembling.

Not just anywhere.

Home.

# Chapter 7: Lina

Silence.

The hum of the fluorescent tube above them. The soft clicking of the microfiche machine.

“Nora.” Theo’s voice shook. “A girl lived in our building. Fifty years ago. And died. The same age as you.”

Nora’s throat tightened. Cold spread through her chest, a cold that had nothing to do with the library’s air-conditioning. Twelve years old. Kirchgasse 14. She could picture the girl. In the kitchen where Nora ate cereal every morning. In the hallway Nora walked through every day. In the room where Nora slept.

“The knocking,” Theo said quietly. “The cold. The word on the window.” He broke off. He didn’t have to say the rest.

“We don’t know that,” Nora said. But there was no fight left in her voice. The words sounded hollow.

“Yes, we do.” Theo looked at her. No jokes. No sarcasm. His eyes were dark and serious. “She lived there. And she’s still there.”

Nora stared at the screen. Six lines. A whole life, summed up in six lines. And then forgotten. For fifty years.

Something heavy settled on Nora’s shoulders. A girl had lived in her building. And maybe nobody had listened to her. She couldn’t shake the thought, even though she didn’t know why yet.

“We have to go home,” she said.

“Home,” Theo repeated. “You mean the apartment where a dead girl—”

“Theo.”

He swallowed. “Okay. Yeah. Home.”

---

Outside, Shadow was lying on the warm sidewalk in front of the library. When he saw them, he jumped up. His tail gave a short wag, then stopped. He nudged Nora’s hand and whined softly.

“What’s wrong with him?” Theo said.

“No idea.” Nora knelt down and stroked Shadow’s head. The dog pressed his nose against her arm. He looked up at her, alert and watching.

“Maybe he can feel it,” Theo murmured. “That we found out something.”

“Dogs can’t sense newspaper articles.”

“This dog can sense ghosts. Newspaper articles are the smaller miracle.”

Then Shadow went stiff under her hand. His ears pricked up. He turned his head and looked across the street.

Toward the cemetery.

Old walls. Ivy. Gravestones behind a wrought-iron fence. The cemetery was right across from the library.

Shadow stared at it. Motionless. His hackles rose.

“Shadow?” Theo said quietly.

The dog growled. Low. Deep. Then he shook himself, as if he wanted to throw something off. His fur lay flat again. He looked away from the cemetery, back at Nora.

Nora shivered. Just for a second. Like an ice cube on the back of her neck.

She untied Shadow from the bike rack.

“Let’s go home,” she said.

“Best idea all day,” Theo said.

They set off. Nora took one last look at the cemetery. Between the gravestones—had something moved? Gray. Fast. Then it was gone.

She walked faster. But she could still feel the cemetery. On her left, behind the wall. Like something waiting.

Shadow trotted along beside her. Close. Closer than usual. His fur brushed her leg with every step.

She stopped in front of the building. Gray walls. Narrow windows. She had looked at this building again and again since yesterday. But now she saw it differently. Now she knew who had died here.

“Nora?” Theo said.

“Yeah.”

“Are you going to stand here till it gets dark, or are we going in?”

She went in. Up the stairs. Opened the apartment door.

Mom was standing in the hallway, sorting the mail. She looked up. “You two look worn out. Where were you?”

“The library,” Nora said.

Mom nodded. Then, casually: “The neighbor told me today that the old woman upstairs is still missing. Silber, I think her name was. Gone for months. Weird, huh?”

Nora could feel Theo’s eyes on her back. She swallowed. “Does the neighbor know her?”

Mom shook her head. “Barely. Apparently she was always alone. With a dog.” She added a letter to the pile. “Kind of strange, right?”

Then she yawned. “I’ve been on my feet since five. I’m going to bed early tonight. There’s food in the fridge.”

The bedroom door clicked shut behind her.

Shadow went ahead. Down the hallway, into the living room, into the kitchen. He sniffed the corners. His fur lay flat.

Nora went into the kitchen. Automatically. She wanted a glass of water. Her throat was as dry as paper.

Then she saw the window.

Condensation. Again. The window was fogged up. In the afternoon. In summer. Impossible. But there it was.

New letters. Two words this time. Clear and sharp. As if written with a child’s finger.

DON’T FORGET

Nora’s glass slipped out of her hand. It crashed onto the tiles and broke into three pieces. She barely noticed. She stood in front of the window and stared at the words.

Don’t forget.

Her breath came way too fast. Her arms went heavy, as if someone had hung weights on them.

“Theo,” she said, her voice rough. “Come here.”

He came. Saw the window. This time he didn’t go pale. This time he just nodded. Slowly. As if he had expected it.

“She’s talking to us,” he said quietly. “Lina is talking to us.”

Don’t forget. Nora didn’t touch the window. This time she didn’t wipe anything away. She just stood there and read the words. Over and over.

Don’t forget. As if Lina had waited fifty years for someone to find her story. And now that they had, she was begging: Don’t forget me. Not again. Not one more time.

Nora’s eyes burned. She blinked. Once. Twice.

“We won’t forget you,” she whispered.

The words stayed on the window. Silent. Still. Like an echo from another time.

Theo came and stood next to her. His arm touched hers. He didn’t say anything. He just stood there. Sometimes that was enough.

“We’ll find out what happened,” Nora said. Her voice was quiet, but steady. “We’ll find out.”

---

Then they heard it. From the living room.

A scratching sound. Soft. Steady. Claws on plaster.

Nora turned around. Theo followed her.

Shadow was sitting in the living room. Right in the middle of the long wall. In front of the old flowered wallpaper. He sat upright. His back was straight. His ears pointed forward.

He was scratching at the wall with his right paw. Slowly. Always in the same spot. Not frantic. Not nervous. Calm and determined. As if he knew exactly what he was doing.

“Shadow?” Theo said.

The dog didn’t react. He kept scratching. His claws left thin lines in the old wallpaper.

“What’s he doing?” Theo whispered.

Nora went closer. Shadow lifted his head for a moment. His eyes met hers. Then he looked down again and kept scratching. In the same spot. Over and over.

In exactly the same spot.

A tingle ran over Nora’s skin. She put her hand on the wall, right next to Shadow’s paw. The wallpaper was old and rough under her fingers. Underneath, she could feel something. A crack. A bump that didn’t belong there.

“There’s something behind the wall,” Nora said.

Theo took a step back. “In movies, that’s usually the line right before everybody screams.”

Shadow kept scratching. Patiently. Without stopping. In exactly the same spot.

As if he wanted to show them where they had to look.

# Chapter 8: Behind the Wallpaper

Shadow scratched at the wall. Always in the same spot. His paw ran over the wallpaper, once, twice, three times. Then he paused, looked at Nora, and kept scratching.

Nora knelt down next to him. The ceiling light cast a dim glow over the old flowered wallpaper.

“What’s wrong with him?” Theo said. He was standing three steps behind her, his arms crossed over his chest.

“He’s scratching the wall.”

“I can see that, Nora. Why is he scratching the wall?”

Nora ran her fingers over the spot. The wallpaper was old. Yellowish, with faded flowers you could hardly make out anymore. But here, in this one spot, it felt different. Thinner. More brittle. As if someone had barely glued it on.

Shadow nudged Nora’s hand with his nose. His eyes were calm. No growling. No raised hackles. He sat next to her and watched, as if he wanted to say: There. Right there.

“He wants us to take a look,” Nora said.

“Take a look. Behind the wallpaper.” Theo shifted from one foot to the other. “Normally people look behind wallpaper when they want to redecorate. Not because their ghost-sniffing dog orders them to.”

“He’s not giving orders. He’s showing us.”

“Even better. Now our dog is a GPS for creepy stuff.”

Nora ignored him. She slid her fingernail under the edge of the wallpaper. The paper came loose right away. No resistance. No glue holding it. The wallpaper came off like skin peeling.

Behind it, the wall was gray and rough. And a crack ran right through the middle of the plaster. Thin, but deep. It ran straight down, about a foot long. The edges were crumbly.

“There’s a crack,” Nora said.

Theo leaned forward. “Old plaster. The house is a hundred years old. Everything cracks.”

Nora shook her head. She laid her hand flat on the wall. Under her fingers, she could feel something. The surface wasn’t even. Next to the crack, the plaster was rougher. Grainier. Different from the rest.

“That’s not a normal crack, Theo. Somebody bricked this up.”

“Bricked it up?”

“Yeah. There used to be an opening here. Somebody sealed it with mortar. And then put wallpaper over it.”

Theo stared at the wall. His mouth was a thin line. “And Shadow smelled that?”

Nora looked at the dog. Shadow had sat down. He sat upright next to the spot, his head tilted a little. Patient. Calm. His eyes went from Nora to the wall and back.

“I think he knew,” Nora said quietly.

Silence.

Theo swallowed. “What do we do now?”

Nora tapped carefully on the mortar. It sounded hollow. There was an empty space under the surface. Small, but there.

“We open it,” she said.

“With what? Our fingernails?”

They needed tools.

“Didn’t you say there were tools in one of the moving boxes?” she said.

Theo’s eyes went wide. “That’s right. Dad’s old toolbox. Mom packed it in the box labeled BASEMENT.”

“Go get it.”

“You want me to walk through the dark apartment alone right now and look for a toolbox?”

“The box is in the storage closet. Ten feet from here.”

“Ten very long, very dark feet.”

“Theo.”

“Yeah, yeah. I’m going.”

He disappeared into the hallway. Nora heard his footsteps, then boxes being shoved around. A dull thud. A muttered “Ow.” Then silence.

Shadow didn’t move. He sat next to Nora and watched the wall. His breathing was calm. Even.

Nora put her hand on his back. His fur was warm. No cold. No whining. Whatever was behind the wall, Shadow wasn’t afraid of it. He wanted them to find it.

Theo’s footsteps came back. He appeared in the doorway with a small red toolbox in his hands. A big grin on his face.

“Hammer, screwdriver, pliers, and something that’s either a chisel or a very aggressive spoon.” He flipped the box open and set it on the floor. “Never say I’m not handy.”

“You’re handy.”

“Thank you. Finally, some appreciation.” He handed her the screwdriver. “You do it. My hands are shaking too much.”

Nora set the tip against the edge of the crack and pushed. The mortar crumbled right away. Pieces fell to the floor, dry and gray.

“It’s practically falling apart on its own,” Nora murmured.

She worked her way along the crack. Piece by piece, the mortar broke away. Behind it, a brick appeared. Red. Small. Set in crooked, as if someone had been in a hurry.

“Give me the hammer.”

Theo handed it to her. “Careful. If the whole wall comes down, I’m telling Mom you started it.”

Nora set the screwdriver against the edge of the brick and tapped it lightly with the hammer. Once. Twice. On the third tap, the brick came loose. It tipped inward and fell into the darkness behind it with a dull clunk.

Behind them, the door to the hallway slammed shut.

The slam was so loud that Nora flinched. Theo screamed. Shadow leaped to his feet and growled. Once. Short. Sharp.

Nora spun around. Her first thought was Mom. But behind the bedroom door, everything stayed quiet. Mom was sleeping the way she had slept since the move. Like a rock.

The door to the hallway was closed. It had been open. The whole time. No draft. No open window.

“That—” Theo started. His voice was thin.

Nora went to the door and grabbed the handle. The door opened. Behind it, the empty hallway. Nothing.

Blood rushed in her ears. Cold air was streaming out of the hole in the wall. It smelled like dust and something old. Like a book that had been lying in a basement for decades.

“There’s an empty space back there,” Nora whispered.

Theo stood close behind her. She could feel his quick breath on the back of her head. “How big?”

Nora pointed her phone’s light into the hole. The beam fell on gray stone. A small space, barely bigger than a shoebox. No pipes. No wires. Someone had built it into the wall on purpose. And closed it up again on purpose.

And there was something inside.

Nora could feel her pulse in her throat. Her fingers clenched around the phone. In the phone’s light, she saw something rectangular. Dusty. Covered with something that might once have been cloth. Gray and thin as cobwebs.

“There’s something in there,” she said.

“What?” Theo’s voice was barely more than a croak.

“I don’t know. Something small.”

Nora reached into the hole. Her fingers touched the thing. Cold. Hard. She closed her fingers around it and pulled it out.

Dust trickled onto the floor. The gray cloth fell away. And in Nora’s hand was a book.

Nora gripped it tighter. A tingle ran through her hands. Not cold. Something else. As if she were holding something that had been waiting a long time for her.

The ceiling light flickered. Only briefly, barely a second. But the shadows on the wall twitched and stretched, grew long and thin like fingers. Nora felt a humming in her ears. Soft, deep, like a tuning fork behind her temples.

The book was small. Barely bigger than her palm. Dark brown leather, cracked and dry. No title. Nothing written on it.

Nora turned it over. A shiver ran down her back.

“A book,” Theo said. “In the wall. Behind mortar and wallpaper. That’s normal. Totally normal.”

Nora opened the cover. The pages were yellowed. Handwritten. A girl’s handwriting. Neat and round, with little circles for the dots on the i’s. Purple ink, smudged in some places.

There were two words on the first page. Centered. Underlined. In big, careful letters.

*Lina’s Diary*

Underneath, a little heart, in the same purple ink.

Nora’s breath caught.

“Theo,” she whispered. “It’s hers. It belongs to Lina.”

Theo leaned over her shoulder. His face was pale.

“Lina’s Diary,” he read. “As in Lina Vogt? As in the girl who, right here—”

“Yeah.”

“In the wall.” Theo swallowed. “Somebody hid her diary in the wall.”

“Or somebody put it there so it would be found.” Nora looked over at Shadow. “By the right person.”

Shadow lifted his head. He looked at Nora. Then at the book. Then at Nora again. His eyes shone in the dim light from the ceiling.

Lina didn’t want to be forgotten. And somebody had hidden her diary in the wall. Bricked it up. As if nobody was ever supposed to find it.

But Shadow had found it.

Nora’s fingers were stiff with nerves as she turned the first page. The writing was small and tight. Purple ink on yellowed paper. There was a date in the top left corner.

March 12, 1974.

Nora began to read.

And right at that moment, the room went cold.

Not slowly. Not bit by bit. All at once. As if someone had yanked open the door of a freezer. The temperature in the room dropped so fast that Nora’s skin hurt. Her breath came out of her mouth in a white cloud.

The light bulb above them flickered. Once. Twice. The light jumped, went dim, came back, went dim. Shadows leaped across the walls, long and thin and trembling.

Shadow whined.

Soft, high, pitiful. He pressed himself against Nora’s leg and whined. His whole body was trembling.

Theo grabbed Nora’s arm. His fingers were ice-cold. His eyes were huge.

“Nora,” he whispered. That was all he could get out.

# Chapter 9: The Diary

The cold hit Nora like a punch.

She sat on the floor, the diary on her knees. The light bulb flickered, then steadied. Shadow whined next to Theo.

Nora’s pulse throbbed in her temples. A girl’s handwriting on the first page. Purple ink.

*Lina’s Diary*

“It got ice-cold,” Theo whispered. He rubbed his arms. “When you opened it. Right that second.”

“I know.” Nora swallowed. The cold crept through her fingers and up into her wrists. But she didn’t put the book down.

Shadow whined once more. Then he went quiet. He lay down right next to Theo’s knee, his head on his paws. His amber eyes were open. Alert. But calm. As if he knew something important was happening.

“Should I read it out loud?” Nora said.

Theo nodded. He sat across from her, his knees pulled up, his back against the wall. His face was pale under the ceiling light.

Nora turned to the first entry.

---

“*March 12, 1974*,” she read aloud. “*New diary! Mom gave it to me for my birthday. Purple ink, because that’s my favorite color. I’m twelve now.*”

Nora stopped. Twelve. Like her.

“*School was boring today*,” she read on. “*Math. Mrs. Kettner got mad at me again because I was daydreaming. But after school was fun. Hanna and I were down by the river. Luna jumped in the water and brought back a stick. Three times. Then she shook herself off and got us wet.*”

“Luna?” Theo said.

“Her dog.” Nora turned the page. “She writes about Luna a lot.”

Shadow lifted his head. His ears perked up.

The next entries were short. School days, friends, cotton candy at the fair. One entry was about the roof. Lina had gone up there with a flashlight to count stars. She had drawn the constellations, tiny dots in purple ink, joined by wobbly lines. Next to them she had written: “When I grow up, I want to be an astronomer. Or a vet. Or both.” An ordinary girl’s life. With every entry, Nora could picture Lina a little better.

“She sounds nice,” Theo said quietly. “Cotton candy. Stars. A dog.” He stroked Shadow’s head. “At least she had a dog.”

“Yeah.” Nora’s voice was rough. “She sounds like someone you’d want to know.”

She kept turning pages: summer, the pool, ice cream from the corner store. Then it was September.

---

“*September 14*,” Nora read. “*Something strange happened. My room was ice-cold this morning. Everywhere else was normal. Only my room. Dad says the window must have been open. It wasn’t.*”

Nora paused. The hair on the back of her neck stood up.

“*September 17. The cold room again. Mom turned up the heat. Didn’t help. And at night Luna sat outside my door and growled. She never growls.*”

Theo sat up. “Luna growled.”

“Yeah.” Nora looked at him. “Outside her room.”

Shadow growled softly. Deep in his throat.

She kept reading. Her voice got quieter.

“*September 22. Noises at night. Scratching in the wall. I woke Mom up. She was annoyed. She said it was mice. But mice don’t scratch to a beat.*”

“To a beat,” Theo repeated. His voice sounded thin. “Like the knocking. At our place.”

Nora nodded. Her tongue stuck to the roof of her mouth.

“*September 29. This morning my desk chair was by the window. I didn’t put it there. Mom says I was sleepwalking. I’ve never walked in my sleep.*”

“Sleepwalking,” Theo muttered. “Sure. Because chairs move by themselves all the time.”

The entries got shorter and shorter. The round letters crowded closer together, as if Lina had written faster. Or as if her hand had been shaking.

---

“*October 5. Luna won’t come into my room. She stands in the doorway and whines. I sleep in the living room with her now.*”

Shadow lifted his head. Only for a moment. Then he laid it back down on his paws. His eyes stayed on Nora.

“Luna acted just like Shadow,” Theo said quietly.

“I know.” Nora swallowed. “Exactly the same.”

“And her parents had an explanation for everything.” Theo shook his head. “Like you did with the pipes.”

Nora said nothing. Because he was right.

She kept reading.

“*October 10. I told Hanna. She laughed. She says I watch too many scary movies. I don’t watch scary movies. I live in one.*”

Theo pulled his knees tighter to his chest. “Her best friend,” he murmured. “Even she didn’t believe her.”

“*October 15*,” Nora read. Her voice was barely a whisper now. “*Dad says it’s my imagination. Mom says it’s my imagination. Hanna says it’s my imagination. But I’m not imagining it. At night someone stands in my room. I can’t see anyone. But I can feel it. Cold. Right next to my bed. And Luna howls in the hallway.*”

Silence. Shadow pressed his head harder against Theo’s knee. Theo put his hand on Shadow’s neck.

Nora turned the page. The paper shook in her hands.

“*October 19. I’m scared. I’m really scared. Nobody believes me. I asked Mom to let me sleep at Grandma’s. She said I should stop making such a big deal out of it. ‘There’s no such thing as ghosts,’ she said. ‘Stop it.’*”

Theo’s chin trembled. Nora saw it. She heard his breath catch. But she kept reading. She had to keep reading.

“*October 23. Last night the door opened by itself. Slowly. I watched it. Nobody was standing there. But the cold came in like a wave. Luna screamed. Really screamed. Like a person.*”

Nora closed her eyes. Just for a moment. Behind her eyelids she saw a girl. Twelve years old. Alone in a dark room.

“Theo?”

“Yeah.”

“I’m sorry.” Her voice cracked. “For not believing you. When you said something was wrong. I didn’t even let you finish. Just like Lina’s parents.”

Theo was quiet. Then he said, “If you’d believed me, I probably would’ve been even more scared.”

Almost a laugh. Almost.

A hot stab went through her chest. Guilt. Hot and sharp.

Nora opened her eyes. Her hands were ice-cold. A weight sat in her chest, heavy and tight.

---

“*October 27*,” she read. Her voice almost broke. “*I’m writing this down because nobody else listens to me. If something happens to me, somebody should know that I told the truth. I wasn’t dreaming. I wasn’t making a big deal out of it. There’s something in this apartment. And it’s getting worse.*”

Theo sobbed.

One short sob. He pressed his hand over his mouth and turned his head away. His shoulders shook.

Nora lowered the book into her lap. “Theo—”

“Keep reading,” he whispered. His voice was all choked up. “Please. Keep reading.”

Shadow pressed his nose harder against Theo’s arm. Theo’s fingers dug into Shadow’s fur. He held on. As if Shadow were an anchor.

Nora’s eyes burned. She blinked the sting away. She had to be the strong one now.

Theo wiped his face. “Nora.” His voice was small. “I want to go home.”

“We are home, Theo.”

He shook his head. But he didn’t say anything else.

She picked the book back up.

---

“Only two entries left,” Nora said quietly. “*October 30.*” She could feel it. The end was coming. The last pages.

“*I’ve been thinking. The basement is the coldest place. Luna won’t go down the basement stairs. She shakes all over and drags me away. But I think the answer is down there. It started down there.*”

Nora turned to the last page with writing on it.

The handwriting was different. Bigger. Shaky. In some places the purple ink was smeared, as if drops had fallen on the paper. Tears.

“*November 2*,” Nora read. Her voice was a whisper.

“*Tomorrow I’m going down to the basement. I have to know what’s down there. Luna is coming with me. I’m scared. But I have to know. If I understand it, maybe it will stop. If I understand it, maybe everybody else will believe me.*”

Nora turned the page.

Empty.

The next page was blank. White. Untouched.

And the one after that. And the one after that.

Lina never wrote again. That was her last entry. On November 2, 1974. The day before she died.

The silence was so thick that Nora could feel it on her skin. Heavy. Cold.

Theo was crying. Quietly, without sobbing. The tears just kept coming. He didn’t wipe them away. He sat there, one hand in Shadow’s fur, and the tears dripped onto his knees.

“She was all alone,” he whispered. “Nobody believed her. And then she went down there. Alone.”

Nora’s throat squeezed shut. She hugged the diary close. A girl. Twelve years old. Purple ink. And no one to believe her.

Shadow lay still between them. His warm body. His calm breathing. He just lay there, as if he knew they needed him.

Nora opened the diary to the first page and ran her hand over it. Over the name. Over the little heart.

“We heard you, Lina,” she whispered. “Someone listened to you.”

Theo swallowed hard. Then he wiped his face with his sleeve. His eyes were red.

“Nora.”

“Yeah.”

He looked at her. No jokes. No sarcasm. Just a ten-year-old boy with wet eyes.

“She went down to the basement.”

“Yeah.”

“And she didn’t come back up.”

Nora’s stomach tightened. The cold in the apartment seemed to grow thicker. Tighter.

Theo looked her straight in the eye.

“What’s in the basement, Nora?”

Shadow lifted his head. His ears turned forward. Under their feet, deep in the building, something creaked.

Nora closed the diary. Her hands were stiff.

“That,” she said quietly, “is what we’ve got to find out.”

# Chapter 10: The Basement

The basement stairs were steep. Nora pointed her phone’s flashlight down the stairs. The beam slid over gray steps. Moisture glistened on the stone.

“I’ll go first,” she said.

“Of course you’ll go first.” Theo stood behind her, his hands buried in his jacket pockets. “I’m the rear guard. Very important job.”

“You’re scared.”

“Of course I’m scared. We’re going into the basement where a girl died fifty years ago. But please, after you.”

Shadow pushed past Nora’s legs. He trotted down the steps. His claws clicked on the stone. His hackles were up, but his steps were calm. He just kept going.

“The dog’s braver than both of us put together,” Theo muttered.

Nora followed Shadow. The air got cooler with every step. The smell changed. Upstairs it smelled like wood and old carpet. Down here it smelled like wet stone and dirt.

The stairs ended at a heavy wooden door. Old wood, swollen with moisture. Nora pushed down on the handle. The door dragged open, with a creak that echoed through the basement. Behind it, everything was dark.

Nora’s phone lit up a long corridor. Concrete floor. Pipes on the ceiling. Storage lockers with padlocks. Old bikes. Boxes in the corners.

“Looks like a normal basement,” Theo said. His voice echoed off the walls. “Creepy, but normal. I don’t see any ghosts. Good sign.”

Shadow kept going. Past the storage lockers, past the old bike, past the boxes. His body was tense. His nose was working.

“Where’s he going?” Theo murmured.

“To the old part,” Nora said.

She had seen it on moving day. The front part of the basement had been redone. Concrete, fluorescent tubes, outlets. But farther back, everything changed. That was where the old basement started. The part that belonged to the original house.

The corridor bent sharply to the left. And suddenly the concrete was gone.

Stone walls. Rough, uneven, damp. The ceiling dropped lower. Her ears filled with pressure. Like diving to the bottom of a swimming pool.

Theo stopped. “You know the part in the movie where somebody says, ‘Come on, let’s go down to the basement’? And everybody in the theater screams NO?” He swallowed. “I’m one of the people in the theater.”

“We’re not in a movie.”

“True. In a movie, at least there’s background music. That way you know when it’s getting dangerous.”

Nora kept going. Three small rooms, connected by low doorways. Cobwebs on the ceiling. Somewhere, something was dripping. In an even rhythm. Like a slow heartbeat. There was a metallic taste on her tongue. Like old coins.

The phone light flickered. Once. Then it went out.

Darkness. Total darkness. All Nora could hear was the dripping. And Theo’s breathing, right next to her ear. His hand found her sleeve and clamped on.

She tapped the screen. Nothing. She tapped again.

Then the light came back. Shaky. Weaker than before.

“Battery?” Theo whispered.

Nora checked the battery. Eighty percent.

She didn’t say anything.

Shadow stopped.

In the middle of the last room. In front of a corner. He stood there, head down, tail between his legs. And then he made a sound Nora had never heard him make. Not like this.

He whined.

Not the short, high whine he had made over the diary. This was different. Deep. Long. A sound that came from his whole body. As if he were in pain.

Nora’s skin tightened. Like an electric shock.

“Shadow?” Theo’s voice shook. “What’s wrong, buddy?”

The dog kept whining. He didn’t back away. He didn’t run. He stood in front of the corner and whined, as if he were grieving.

Nora lifted the phone. The beam fell on the wall.

A weight pressed down on her chest.

Cracks. Everywhere. Dozens of thin lines running across the stone. But not natural cracks. These lines were parallel. In groups. Sets of four, side by side.

Like fingers. Like someone had scratched at the stone with their fingers.

Nora tasted something sour in her mouth. The scratches were old. Worn smooth by decades of moisture. Fifty years old.

“Nora.” Theo’s voice was barely a whisper. He was standing right behind her. His hand was still clutching her sleeve. “Those are finger marks.”

“Yeah.”

“Somebody scratched at this wall.”

Nora just nodded.

Nothing but the dripping. And Shadow, whining softly now, almost too quiet to hear.

Theo swallowed loud enough for Nora to hear. “It happened down here,” he whispered. “Whatever happened to Lina. It was down here.”

Nora didn’t answer. She stared at the scratch marks. Her arms hung heavy at her sides. Something was spreading through her chest. Not fear. Something else. Something deeper. Sadness. So heavy she could hardly breathe.

A girl. Twelve years old. Alone in this basement. Fingers scratching at stone.

“I’m cold,” Theo said. He rubbed his arms. “Really cold. Not normal cold.”

He was right. The temperature had dropped. Slowly. Bit by bit. As if the cold were seeping out of the walls.

Nora could see her breath. A white cloud in front of her face. She raised her hand and watched the fine hairs on her forearm stand up.

“Theo. Do you see that?”

He nodded. She could see his breath too. “That’s not the pipes, is it?”

“No.”

Shadow stopped whining. Suddenly. Just like that. He lifted his head and looked at Nora. His eyes were wide. There was something in them that Nora couldn’t place. Not fear. Not a warning.

Grief.

The dog looked sad. As if he knew something he couldn’t say.

And then Nora felt it.

It came without warning. No sound. No light. Just a feeling. Right behind her. Inches away.

Someone was standing behind her.

Nora couldn’t see anyone. But she could FEEL it. Like eyes on the back of her neck. The air behind her was thicker. Colder. Heavier.

Goosebumps exploded over her whole body. Every nerve in her screamed: TURN AROUND.

But she couldn’t move.

“Nora?” Theo’s voice sounded far away. “You look really pale.”

“Someone’s there,” she whispered. “Behind me.”

Theo’s eyes went wide. He looked over her shoulder. His face scrunched up. Not from fear. From confusion.

“There’s nobody there, Nora.”

“Yes, there is.”

“I don’t see anyone.”

“Me neither.” Her voice was a whisper. “But she’s there.”

Shadow stood up. Slowly. He came over to Nora and pressed himself against her leg. Warm. Solid. Real. His body wasn’t trembling anymore. He just stood there. Between Nora and whatever was behind her.

The presence changed. The cold grew softer. Not warmer, but less sharp. Like a hand opening. Like fast breathing slowing down.

Then it was over.

The thick air behind Nora’s back faded away. Her shoulders dropped. Until now, she hadn’t even noticed how tense she’d been.

“Okay,” Theo said. His voice was thin and high. “Okay. That was definitely something.” He took a breath. “New rule: no more basements. Never again. Not even to get a soda.” He swallowed. “Can we please go now?”

Nora turned around. Slowly. Her neck cracked softly.

The corner was empty. Just damp stone and cold air.

But the floor wasn’t.

There were tracks in the dust. Footprints. Small. Narrow. Bare feet. A child’s feet.

They started at the corner with the scratch marks. They led right up behind the spot where Nora had just been standing. Five steps. Maybe six. Every print clear in the gray dust. Toes, ball, heel. Perfect.

They weren’t theirs. Nora and Theo were wearing sneakers. These were bare feet. A child’s feet.

Theo saw them too. He took a step back. His mouth opened. No sound came out. His eyes were huge.

Shadow lowered his head and sniffed the nearest print. Then he looked up. At Nora. With that look that said he knew too much.

The footprints ended right behind Nora. Exactly where she had felt the presence. Exactly where someone she couldn’t see had been standing.

Lina had been here.

Nora reached for Theo’s hand. He held on tight. His fingers were ice-cold. Behind them, in the corner, the water kept dripping. Steady. Calm. As if nothing had happened.

But the footprints stayed in the dust.

“Run,” Nora whispered.

# Chapter 11: Signs on the Wall

Nora ran.

The basement steps flew by under her feet. Her lungs burned. Behind her, she heard Theo’s quick footsteps and Shadow’s claws on the stone. Higher. Faster. Away from the footprints. Away from the cold. Away from whatever had been waiting for them down there.

She yanked open the basement door and stumbled into the hallway. Daylight. Warm air. Dust in the sunbeams.

Nora stopped. She put her hands on her knees and breathed. Her pulse roared in her ears.

Theo came out behind her. Shadow close at his side. The basement door slammed shut behind them. The sound echoed through the stairwell like a gunshot.

“That’s it,” Nora said. Her voice sounded strange. Rough. “We quit.”

Theo stared at her. “What?”

Nora straightened up. Her fingers tingled. She crossed her arms so he wouldn’t see. “That stuff down there—the footprints—it’s too much. We’re kids. This isn’t for us.”

Theo shook his head. Slowly. Firmly.

“No.”

Nora blinked. “No?”

“No.” Theo stood in front of her, his hands balled into fists. His eyes were red from crying. His voice was still shaking. But he didn’t back down. “We can’t quit.”

“Yes, we can. We go upstairs, lock the door, and forget all of it.”

“What about Lina?”

The name hung in the air. Heavy. Still.

A knot formed under Nora’s ribs. She swallowed. “Lina is—”

“Lina is still down there.” Theo’s voice got louder. “Alone. Like she’s been for the last fifty years. And we’re the only ones who know.”

“Theo, I—”

“You read her diary, Nora. You read what she wrote. ‘Nobody believes me.’ ‘If something happens to me.’ You cried.”

“I didn’t cry.”

“Your eyes were wet. That counts.”

Nora pressed her lips together. The cold from the basement was still in her bones. The footprints. Small, bare feet in the dust. A child’s feet.

“Theo, you’re ten. A minute ago in the basement, you were clinging to my sleeve.”

“I’m still scared.” He looked her in the eye. Straight on. Without looking away. “But Lina was scared too. And nobody was with her.”

The only sound was Shadow breathing. Slow and even.

Nora opened her mouth. Closed it again. She couldn’t argue. Not with the truth.

Shadow padded past her. Slow, calm. He lay down at Nora’s feet. Right on top of her shoes. Then he lifted his head and looked at her. His eyes were warm. Patient. He rested his nose on the toes of her shoes and sighed.

As if he were saying: I’m here. We can do this.

Nora looked at Shadow. At Theo. At the basement door behind them. Her heart was beating fast, but not like before. Not panic. Something else.

“She needs us,” Theo said quietly. “Please, Nora.”

Nora closed her eyes. For a moment, she saw Lina’s diary in front of her. Round letters. Purple ink. The little heart on the first page.

She opened her eyes.

“We’re going back up,” she said. “To Mrs. Silber’s apartment. But this time we search for real.”

Theo grinned. For the first time since the basement. “Since when am I the brave one?”

“Since right now.” Nora bent down and scratched Shadow behind the ear. “Don’t tell anyone.”

“Who would I tell? The ghost?”

“Theo.”

“I’m being quiet.”

---

The door to Mrs. Silber’s apartment was open a crack. Like always. Nora pushed it open. The familiar creak. The smell of old books and candle wax. Cool air, but not ice-cold. Not threatening.

Shadow went ahead. He trotted down the hallway, nose to the floor. No growling. No raised hackles. He sniffed the doorframes, the walls, the stacks of books.

“What exactly are we looking for?” Theo said.

“Anything that tells us if Mrs. Silber helped the ghosts, and how.” Nora went into the study. The list of names on the wall. The burned-down candles on the desk. Books about Gravenstedt’s history. “She did this for years. She must have written down how.”

“Maybe her whole system was just ‘dog growls, lady runs over.’”

“Theo, help me look.”

He held up his hands. “I am looking.”

Nora pulled open the desk drawers. Pens. Candles. Loose notes. Nothing that helped.

Theo was kneeling in front of the stacks of books. He pulled books out and pushed them back in. “Gravenstedt City History. Cemeteries of Northern Germany.” He paused. “Mrs. Silber had really creepy hobbies.” He held up a book. “*Conversations with the Beyond*. She had a hotline.”

“Focus.”

“I’m just saying—”

“Theo.”

He reached for the next book. Pulled it out. Something fell from behind the book onto the floor. A muffled sound. Small. Light.

Theo looked down at the floor. “Something fell out.”

Nora came over to him. A notebook was lying among the dust bunnies on the wooden floor. Small, barely bigger than a postcard. Black cover. Worn. The corners bent.

Nora picked it up. Her fingers tingled.

She opened it. Handwriting. Tight, neat, old-fashioned. Blue ink. There was a name on the first page.

*Margret Silber: Notes*

“It’s her notebook,” Nora whispered.

Theo came and stood next to her. “What does it say?”

Nora flipped through it. Every page was full. Not one empty spot. Mrs. Silber had written everything down. Names, places, dates, observations. In between were little drawings—floor plans of houses, street corners, basements. The ink changed. Blue, then black, then blue again. Years of notes. A whole life in one small notebook.

Nora read aloud. Her voice was quiet in the silent room.

“‘The Restless. That is what I call them. They are here because no one knows their story. They are stuck. Between what was and what could have been.’”

Theo gulped.

Nora turned the page. “‘My method is simple. I find out who they were. What happened to them. Their story. And then I show them that someone remembers. That they are not forgotten. Only then can they go.’”

“That’s it,” Theo said. “That’s what she did.”

Nora nodded. Her heart beat faster. Not from fear. From something else. From understanding.

She kept reading. “‘The Restless need someone who understands their story. Not just knows it. Understands it. Who were they? What did they lose? What is keeping them here? The chain only comes loose when someone truly understands that.’”

Shadow had lain down next to Nora’s feet. Still. Attentive. His eyes followed the pages, as if he could read.

Nora turned the pages. Farther back, she found an entry that looked different. More personal.

“‘Shadow senses them before I do. He always knew where I needed to go. When his hackles rise, one of them is here. When he whines, the ghost is sad. When he growls, the ghost is angry. He was my compass. For forty years.’”

Theo knelt down next to Shadow and put a hand on the dog’s back. “Forty years. You were her helper.”

Shadow lifted his head. He nudged Theo’s hand with his nose. Quick. Gentle. Then he laid his head back down on his paws.

Nora kept turning pages. Page after page. Names, stories, methods. Every ghost had an entry. Every release was written down. Some were short: “Anna Berger, 1923. Drowned in the canal. Only wanted someone to know her name. Three nights.” Others were long, several pages, with sketches and notes.

Then Nora reached the last page with writing on it.

The handwriting was different. Bigger. Shakier. The letters ran crookedly across the paper, as if Mrs. Silber’s hand had been shaking. Or as if she had written in the dark. By the light of a single candle, in the middle of the night.

Nora read. Slowly. Every word.

“‘I believe I am next.’”

Theo froze.

“‘The Veil is getting thinner. I can feel it. At night, when I close my eyes. Something pulls at me. Quietly. But it is getting stronger.’”

Nora’s fingers went cold. The air in the room grew thicker. Heavier. Like an invisible hand settling on her shoulders. The pressure pushed against her temples.

“‘And I can feel HIM. The First One. He is not like the Restless. He is not waiting. He is searching.’”

Total silence. Nora couldn’t even hear Shadow breathing.

Theo whispered, “Who’s the First One?”

Nora stared at the page. The last words. Shaky blue ink on yellowed paper. After that: nothing. Empty pages. Just like Lina’s diary.

Mrs. Silber never wrote again.

Nora closed the notebook. Her fingers were stiff. The air in the room grew heavier. Thicker. As if something were pressing against the walls from outside.

“I don’t know,” she said quietly. “But Mrs. Silber’s hand was shaking when she wrote about him.”

Shadow stood up. His hackles rose from his neck all the way to his tail. He went to the door. He didn’t trot. He hurried. He looked out into the stairwell, then back at the kids. His whole body was trembling.

Theo swallowed. “The First One. He’s searching.”

“Yeah.”

“And Mrs. Silber stopped writing.”

“Yeah.”

“Just like Lina stopped writing.”

Nora didn’t answer. She hugged the notebook to her chest and stared at the empty pages that came after the last entry.

Who was the First One?

And what had he done to Mrs. Silber?

Somewhere in the apartment, something clicked. Softly. Like a door latching shut.

Shadow growled. He stared down the hallway. Toward the last door. Toward the cold room.

Then they heard it.

Thump. Thump. Thump.

Pause.

Thump. Thump. Thump.

# Chapter 12: Please

Shadow barked.

The sound jolted Nora out of her sleep. Loud. Deep. A single, sharp sound that echoed through the whole apartment.

Nora sat bolt upright in bed. Her whole body was tense. Her hands dug into the covers. Shadow was standing on the floor next to her. Stiff. Upright. Legs locked, hackles up, his eyes fixed on the bedroom door.

He barked again.

Goosebumps prickled over Nora’s skin. In all the time they had known him, Shadow had never barked. Not once. He had growled, whined, refused to go in. But barked? Never.

Until now.

That evening, the knocking had stopped before they worked up the nerve to open the door. Nora had lain awake for a long time.

“Nora!” Theo’s voice came from the hallway. Thin. Scared. “Nora, are you coming?”

She threw back the covers. The cold hit her like a punch. The floor was ice-cold under her bare feet. Her breath came out in a white cloud.

She ran into the hallway. Theo was standing outside his bedroom door, his arms wrapped around himself. He was shivering. Shadow pressed against Nora’s leg and growled softly.

“Look at the windows,” Theo whispered.

Nora turned around. Her fingers went cold.

Every window in the apartment was fogged up. Thick, milky condensation. On the kitchen window. On the hallway window. On the living room window. Everywhere.

And there were words on every window.

DIARY.

READ.

PLEASE.

DON’T FORGET.

As if written with a finger. Big, hurried letters. Water ran down at the edges, but the words stayed.

Nora swallowed. Her throat was tight. The cold crept through her whole body. But it wasn’t just the temperature. The moonlight cast dark shapes on the walls. They were moving. Slowly. Even though nothing in the room stirred. A presence. Stronger than ever before.

“She’s here,” Nora said quietly.

“Yeah.” Theo’s voice cracked. “Nora, I saw something.”

“What?”

He pointed down the hallway. To the end, where the hall turned off toward the bathroom. His finger was shaking.

“A shadow. But not our Shadow. A different one. It was as big as a kid. It was standing there. Just for a second.” Theo swallowed hard. “Then it was gone.”

A cold tingle ran over Nora’s arms. She stared down the dark hallway. Nothing. Only darkness and cold.

Shadow was still growling. Soft, deep, in his throat.

“Did you see a face?” Nora whispered.

“No. Just the outline. Like a figure made of smoke.” Theo rubbed his arms. “Nora, she wrote PLEASE. On every window.”

Nora looked at him. His face was pale in the moonlight. He was shaking all over. But his eyes were serious.

“Do you think Mom heard anything?” he whispered.

Nora listened. Nothing came from Mom’s bedroom. No rustling. No footsteps. Only silence. Mom was sleeping like a rock, the way she always did.

“No,” Nora said.

Nothing but the ticking of the kitchen clock. The words on the windows shimmered in the moonlight. PLEASE. READ. DON’T FORGET.

Theo swallowed. “What do we do?”

Nora took a deep breath. The cold air burned in her lungs. She thought of Mrs. Silber’s notebook. Of the method. Find out the story. Understand it. Show the ghost that someone remembers.

They had read Lina’s diary. But only for themselves. Not for Lina.

“We’re going to help her,” Nora said. “Now. Tonight.”

Theo’s eyes went wide. “How?”

“We’ll read to her,” Nora said. “The diary. Out loud. All of it. Every word. In her room.”

“Her room is your room.”

“Yeah.”

Theo looked at her. Three seconds. Then he nodded. “Okay.”

“Okay?”

“I said okay, Nora. Before I change my mind.” He rubbed his arms. “But afterward I want hot chocolate. With marshmallows. And we never talk about tonight again.”

Nora almost smiled. Almost. She went into her room and took the diary out of her nightstand drawer. The leather cover was cold under her fingers.

Shadow followed her. He wasn’t barking anymore. He didn’t growl. He walked beside her, close against her leg, and his eyes were bright and calm.

---

They sat down on the floor in Nora’s room. Lina’s room. It was so cold that Nora could barely feel her own fingers. Shadow lay down between them, his head on his paws. Alert. Still.

Theo pulled the comforter off Nora’s bed and wrapped it around his shoulders. “If I freeze to death, put this on my tombstone: He just wanted to help.”

“You’re not going to freeze to death.”

“You sure?”

“No.”

Theo snorted softly. Then he got serious. “Start reading.”

Nora opened the diary. Her fingers were stiff with cold. But the shaking didn’t come from the cold. It came from something bigger.

She stopped. What if it didn’t work? What if it made everything worse? What if the cold came back stronger than they could stand?

Theo looked at her. Waiting. Trusting.

Nora swallowed. Then she made up her mind. For Lina.

She began to read.

This time she wasn’t reading for herself. Not for Theo. She was reading for the room. For the cold. For the girl who had slept here before it was Nora’s room.

She read all of it. Every word. The birthday and the purple ink. Luna fetching a stick out of the river. The stars on the roof. She could see her breath above the paper.

Then came the dark pages. The cold room. The scratching in the wall. Lina’s mom, who said *imagination*.

The cold got stronger. Not weaker. Stronger.

Frost spread across the window glass in feathery patterns. The pages went stiff under Nora’s fingers. Theo pulled the comforter up to his nose.

“Nora,” he whispered. “It’s getting worse.”

Nora’s voice got quieter. But she didn’t stop.

Something changed.

It happened so slowly that Nora didn’t notice at first. Her fingers weren’t numb anymore. The air didn’t bite quite as hard. The cold gave way. Not all at once. Like a block of ice melting. Drop by drop.

“*Nobody believes me*,” Nora read.

She read the sentence a second time. Slower. So it would stay in the room.

Theo sniffled. Shadow lifted his head. He looked at a spot next to the bed where nobody was sitting. Then he laid his head back down.

Nora kept reading. Page after page. With every sentence, the cold gave way a little more. Like a wave sliding back from the beach.

Then she reached the last page with writing on it. The shaky letters. The sentence about the basement.

Her voice was only a whisper now. But she read it to the end.

She closed the book.

Silence.

The cold was almost gone. Nora couldn’t see her breath anymore. Her fingers tingled. Feeling came back, slowly, like blood flowing back into hands that had fallen asleep. The air was still cool, but not icy anymore. Like a fall morning. Not like a freezer.

Theo sat there, his knees pulled up to his chest. Tears ran down his cheeks, but he was smiling. A small, shaky smile.

“She was listening,” he whispered. “The whole time.”

Shadow lay still between them. His fur was soft. His body warm. His eyes were half closed. Relaxed. For the first time that night.

Nora hugged the diary to her chest. The leather cover wasn’t cold anymore. It was warm. Hand-warm, as if someone had held it for a long time.

“We heard you, Lina,” Nora said into the silent room. “We know your story. You weren’t alone.”

Nothing happened. No sound. No wind. No flickering.

But something came loose. Like a knot coming undone. Like a breath going out at last. The heaviness was gone.

Theo leaned back against the wall. “I think she’s crying,” he whispered.

Nora nodded. Her own eyes burned. She blinked the tears away.

Shadow sighed deeply. One long dog sigh. Then he closed his eyes all the way.

---

Minutes went by. Maybe longer. Nora didn’t know.

Then she stood up. Her legs were stiff. She went to the window. The condensation was almost gone. The words had faded. READ. PLEASE. DON’T FORGET. All gone. Erased, as if the warmth had wiped them away.

But there, on the last strip of condensation at the bottom of the glass, was a new word.

THANK YOU.

Nora’s breath caught. She put her fingertips against the glass. It was warm. Not cold. Warm.

“Theo,” she whispered. “Come here.”

Theo stood next to her. He read it. His chin trembled.

“You’re welcome, Lina,” he murmured. He rubbed his arms. “Next time, maybe not at three in the morning.”

Nora smiled.

Then a second word appeared right next to it. Letter by letter. As if an invisible finger were writing on the glass.

UP.

Nora stared at the word. Her smile vanished. Something cold stabbed through her chest. Up. Mrs. Silber’s apartment was right above them. On the third floor.

Theo looked at her. His eyes were wide. “What’s upstairs, Nora?”

Nora didn’t answer. She held on tight to the diary and stared at the words in the condensation.

THANK YOU. UP.

Shadow lifted his head. His eyes went to the ceiling. His tail tapped the floor once. Then he was still again.

# Chapter 13: The Release

Up.

Nora stared at the word in the condensation. The letters were clear and sharp. No doubt about it. Not one shaky line. Next to it, THANK YOU was still there.

“Up,” Theo said. He was standing next to her, hugging the diary to his chest. Sometime during the night, Nora had given it to him. He hadn’t let go of it since. “She means Mrs. Silber’s apartment.”

Nora nodded. Lina had lived down here, in what was now Nora’s room. But the cold and the knocking had always come from above. From the empty apartment. As if Lina had hidden away up there, where nobody would bother her.

Her heart beat steadily. For the first time in days, it beat steadily. The cold had left the apartment. The air felt the way it does after a thunderstorm. Fresh. Soft. As if someone had opened a window.

Shadow stood at the apartment door. He didn’t scratch. He didn’t whine. He just waited. His tail wagged slowly back and forth.

“Then let’s go up,” Nora said.

Theo looked down at the diary in his hands. At the leather cover. At the first page, where the name was written in a girl’s handwriting.

“Should I bring it?” he said.

Nora thought about it. The diary belonged to Lina. It had been lying in a hole in the wall for fifty years. Forgotten. Hidden. Now someone had read it. Someone had listened.

“Yeah,” she said. “Bring it.”

---

The stairwell was silent. Their footsteps echoed on the wooden stairs. Nora went first, Theo right behind her. Shadow walked between them, his claws clicking softly on the wood.

Third floor. The door to Mrs. Silber’s apartment was open a crack. Like always. As if it were waiting for them.

Nora pushed it open. No creak. The door swung inward without a sound. Shadow went in first. No hesitating. No growling. He trotted through the doorway like it was the most natural thing in the world.

“Okay,” Theo muttered. “If the dog goes in, we go in.”

The hallway was dark. Moonlight came in through the kitchen window at the end of the hall and painted a silver stripe on the floorboards. Nora smelled dust and old wood. But the air wasn’t cold. Not like before. Not like the first time.

“It’s warm,” Theo whispered. “Nora, it’s warm up here.”

“I know.”

She walked down the hallway. Past the kitchen. Past the room with the notes and the list of names. All the way to the end of the hall. All the way to the door they had never wanted to go through. The cold room. The room Shadow had stayed away from on the first day. He had planted himself sideways across the hallway in front of it. He hadn’t let them in.

The door was open.

Nora stopped. Her fingers tingled. But not from fear. It was a different kind of tingling. Like excitement. Like the moment before you open a present.

“Wait here,” she said quietly.

Theo’s eyes went wide. “What? No. Nora—”

“Just one minute.”

Before he could argue, Nora stepped through the door. Alone.

The air in the room closed around her. Cool, but not cold. Like someone holding their breath. Nora could feel her heartbeat all the way to her fingertips. She stood in the dark and felt the presence. Close. Right in front of her. Everywhere.

“It’s me,” Nora whispered. “I read your diary.”

Nothing happened. But the air got softer. Warmer. As if someone were letting their shoulders drop.

Then Shadow came. He squeezed past Nora’s leg through the door. Without hesitating. Without growling. He trotted into the room as if he had done it a thousand times. He turned around once in a circle, the way dogs do before they lie down.

Then he lay down. In the middle of the room. His head on his paws.

And sighed.

“He’s about to start snoring,” Theo whispered from the doorway. But his voice was shaking.

Nora’s breathing went shallow. Something pressed behind her ribs. Warm. Deep.

Theo stepped up beside her. The room was small. One window that looked out onto Kirchgasse. No furniture. Bare floorboards. Empty walls. Moonlight on the floor.

And something else.

---

A spot of light. Small. Round. Like a sunbeam shining through a leaf. It lay on the floor, a few steps in front of Shadow. Golden. Warm. It moved slowly, the way sunlight moves across a wall.

But it was night. The moon was shining silver. No sun. No lamp. Nothing that could explain this light.

Nora’s breath caught.

The light pulsed. Gently. Like a heartbeat. Brighter, then dimmer, in a slow, calm rhythm.

It was the most beautiful thing Nora had ever seen.

“Theo,” she whispered. “Do you see that?”

“Yeah.” His voice caught. “Yeah, I see it.” He reached for her hand. “What do we do?”

“Say her name,” Nora whispered. “Tell her we know her.”

Theo swallowed. Then he said quietly, “Lina.”

The light pulsed stronger. Warmer. Nora could feel it on her face. Like sunshine in spring.

“Lina Vogt,” Nora said. “We read your diary. We know who you were.”

Theo knelt down. Very carefully, he laid the diary next to the light. It belonged to her.

Shadow lifted his head. He slowly got up and walked over to the light. Three steps. Then he lay down in front of it and bowed his head. As if he were saying goodbye to someone he had known for a long time.

And for one heartbeat, Nora was Lina. She felt the cold basement floor under bare feet. The fear. The loneliness. Fifty years of waiting. Then it was over. Gone in a blink. Nora was Nora again. But now she understood.

Shadow lay still. His eyes were half closed. His tail lay motionless on the floor. He looked like a dog who had been gone a long time and had finally come home.

“What’s happening?” Theo whispered.

Nora didn’t answer. She couldn’t put it into words. But she could feel it. Something was letting go.

The spot of light moved. Slowly. It slid across the floor, over the cover of the diary, past Shadow’s paw, toward the window. It got a little bigger. A little brighter. The gold turned whiter. Purer.

Nora’s eyes blurred. She didn’t blink.

“She’s going,” Theo whispered.

Two words. That was all it took. Nora knew he was right. She knew it in her bones. In her chest. In the place where you feel things you can’t explain.

Now the light was beaming. Not blinding. Like a candle in a dark room.

Then it got smaller.

“Nora,” Theo whispered. “The light’s getting smaller.”

It shrank. Slowly. Gently. The glow gathered into one single point. Bright. Warm. Perfectly still.

And then it was gone.

---

Nothing but the creaking of the old house.

No cold. No knocking. No scratching. No words in the condensation. Just the normal, ordinary silence of an empty apartment on a quiet street in a sleeping city.

Shadow lay on the floor, breathing evenly. No raised hackles. No tense muscles. Just a dog who was asleep.

Nora noticed that her cheeks were wet. She lifted her hand and wiped them with the back of it. Warm. Salty.

Next to her, Theo sniffled. He wiped his nose on his sleeve and made a sound that was half laughing and half sobbing.

“We just watched a ghost go,” he murmured. “In case my teacher asks on Monday what I did this weekend.”

Nora laughed. Short. Quiet. It felt good. It loosened something in her chest that had been stuck there for days. A knot she hadn’t even noticed.

“Do you think Mrs. Silber did it like this too?” Theo murmured. “Watched them go?”

“Yeah,” Nora said. “I think so. Just like this.”

“For forty years.”

“For forty years.”

Theo didn’t say anything for a moment. Then he said quietly, “That must have been lonely.”

Nora didn’t answer. She thought of the old woman with the white hair. Of the photo in the apartment. Of Shadow, younger, next to her. Forty years. Alone with the dead.

She knelt down next to Shadow and put her hand on his fur. Warm. His chest rose and fell under her fingers. He opened one eye, looked at her, and closed it again.

“Good dog,” Nora whispered. Her voice was rough. “Good, good dog.”

Theo sat down next to her on the floor. He leaned against the wall and stretched out his legs. For a moment they just sat there. In the room that had been ice-cold for months. Now it was as warm as any other room in the house.

Lina was free. The girl with the purple ink and the round letters. The girl nobody had believed. The girl who had gone down to the basement alone.

Someone had listened to her. And she had gone.

“Nora?” Theo looked at the spot where the light had been. “What really happened down there? In the basement?”

In her mind, Nora saw the scratch marks again. The little footprints in the dust. *Tomorrow I’m going down to the basement.*

“I don’t know,” she said. “Just now, when the light was here, all I felt was how it felt for her. Cold. And all alone.”

“You said we’d find out.”

“I did.” She swallowed. “But I don’t think that was the most important thing for her. She never wrote ‘Find out what happened.’”

“Then what?”

“‘Believe me.’”

Theo was quiet for a long time. Then he said, “We did.”

“We did,” Nora said.

“Do you think it hurt?” Theo whispered. “Going, I mean.”

Nora thought about the light. About the warmth. About the slow, calm pulse.

“No,” she said. “I think it was like falling asleep. When you’re really tired and you finally get to let go.”

Theo nodded. He didn’t say anything else. He didn’t need to.

---

After a while Nora stood up. Her knees cracked. The night was still. Moonlight shone through the window onto the floor, right where the golden light had been. Now it was only silver. Cold and clear.

She went to the window. From up here she could see over the rooftops. Gravenstedt spread out in front of her like a blanket of darkness and lights. Streetlights, yellow and warm. Windows where lamps were still on. Chimneys. Antennas. The outline of the church tower at the end of the street.

A normal city. A completely normal city at night. But Nora knew better.

Then she saw it.

Three, maybe four blocks away. Between two roofs. A glow. Quick. Cold. Blue.

Not golden. Not warm. Not like Lina’s light. This was different. Icy. Sharp. Like lightning striking underground.

It flared up once. Two seconds. Maybe three. Then it was gone.

A shiver ran down Nora’s back. Her fingers on the windowsill turned white.

“Theo.”

He came over to her. “What?”

“Did you see that?”

“See what?” Theo rubbed his eyes. “I was looking at the floor.”

Nora stared at the spot between the roofs. Dark. Still. Nothing. As if it had never been there.

But it had been there. Something blue. Something cold.

She thought of Mrs. Silber’s list. Of all the names on it. Of the stories nobody knew. Of the ones who couldn’t go.

Behind her, on the floor, Shadow lifted his head. His eyes gleamed in the moonlight. He looked at the window. At the spot where the blue glow had been.

And growled. Low. Deep.

# Chapter 14: The Map

Nora woke up and didn’t know where she was.

Sunlight came through the window. Warm. Golden. Her room looked like any other bedroom on a summer morning. And for the first time since the move, it felt like one too.

Then the memories came back. The diary. The cold. The light on the floor that got smaller and disappeared. Lina.

Saturday. No school. No knocking in the wall. No icy spots. Just quiet and the smell of coffee from the kitchen.

Shadow was lying at the foot of her bed. He lifted his head, yawned wide, and laid it back down.

“You feel better too, huh?” Nora murmured.

Shadow wagged his tail once.

---

Theo was sitting in the kitchen with a glass of milk. His hair stuck out in every direction. But he was grinning.

“Morning,” he said. “Sleep okay? Without ghosts, I mean?”

“Without ghosts.” Nora sat down across from him. “For the first time.”

“We helped a ghost. A real ghost. Can we put that on our résumé?”

“You don’t have a résumé. You’re ten.”

“Then I’ll start one.” He counted on his fingers. “Freeing ghosts. Teamwork with supernatural dog. Experience reading diaries out loud below freezing.”

Nora laughed. For real. Her shoulders relaxed.

“By the way, I’m not scared of the dark anymore,” Theo said. “What else could happen? I’m immune now.” He paused. “Except to spiders. Spiders are worse than ghosts.”

Shadow lay down under the table. His warm body pressed against Nora’s feet.

---

After breakfast Nora went up the stairs. Third floor. Theo followed her.

“What are you looking for?”

“Mrs. Silber helped ghosts for forty years,” Nora said. “She must have left more than a notebook and a list.”

The door was open, like always. Shadow pushed past them and trotted down the hallway as if the apartment belonged to him. Which it once had.

The apartment smelled like old books and dust. But the cold was gone.

Theo stopped in front of the list of names. At the very bottom: *Lina Vogt? – 1974?* The question marks were still there.

“Mrs. Silber knew about Lina,” he murmured. “But she never managed to help her.”

“No,” Nora said. “But we did.”

They went into the kitchen. Old cabinets, peeling paint, empty shelves. Nora opened one cabinet after another. Nothing but dust.

Shadow wasn’t interested in the cabinets. He was lying in front of the sink, his nose close to the baseboard underneath. He sniffed. Then he pushed his nose into a gap Nora hadn’t seen.

“What have you got there?”

One piece of the baseboard was loose. Nora pulled on it. On the third try, it came free with a soft crunch. Behind it was a thick roll of paper with a rubber band around it.

Nora unrolled it on the kitchen floor. Theo held the corners flat.

It was a map.

---

Hand-drawn. Almost three feet wide. Black ink on yellowed paper. Streets, squares, a river along the edge.

Gravenstedt.

And spread over the whole city: circles. Thick and clear, drawn in black ink. Next to each circle, a name and a year.

Nora counted. Twelve.

A circle at the cemetery. *Heinrich B. – 1887.*

Nora kept her finger on it. Three, maybe four blocks from their building. Right there, between two roofs, the blue light had flickered last night. Or somewhere close by. She wasn’t sure.

But the direction was right.

A circle at an old factory by the river. *Marta K. – 1912.*

A circle in the city park. *Clara B. – 1889.*

“They’re all dead people,” Theo whispered. His face was pale.

“They’re the ghosts,” Nora said. The hair on the back of her neck stood up. “Mrs. Silber found every single one and marked them on the map.”

She ran her finger over the years. 1843. 1889. 1912. Some of the circles were almost as old as the city.

“Twelve,” Theo said. “And we’ve done one.” His lips moved while he did the math. “If every one of them is as scary as Lina, I’ll have gray hair by eleven.”

“You’re ten.”

“Exactly. One year left.”

Then Nora saw the edge of the map.

There was another circle there. But not a black one. A big red circle with a double outline. The ink was thick and dark. A deep red, like old sealing wax.

No name. No date. Just one word in capital letters.

GRAVEN.

Shadow leaned over the map. Then he jerked his head back as if he had burned his nose. He flattened his ears. And turned his back on the red circle.

“Okay,” Theo said. “The dog doesn’t like that word. I don’t like that word either.”

“Graven,” Nora said slowly. “Like Gravenstedt.”

“A person?”

“Maybe. Wait.”

She rolled up the map, tucked it under her arm, and ran into the study. On their second visit, she had seen a book there. It was still lying on the stack next to the candles. *Gravenstedt: A Chronicle.*

Nora opened it to the first chapter. One paragraph. Three sentences.

*Gravenstedt was founded in 1823 by Alwin Graven. Graven built the first buildings of what is now the Old Town. Little is known about the man himself.*

Below it was a footnote in tiny print. *The city archives were lost in a fire in 1871.*

“How convenient,” Theo said dryly. “The man who founded the city. No picture, no story. Just a name. And a red circle.”

Nora thought of the notebook. Of Mrs. Silber’s last, shaky lines. *I can feel HIM. The First One.*

“The First One,” she whispered.

Theo swallowed. “The first ghost? The oldest one?”

“Or the most dangerous.”

Shadow stood in the doorway. He didn’t come in.

---

Back in the kitchen, Nora unrolled the map on the floor again.

Shadow trotted over and sniffed the paper. Very slowly, circle by circle. At one of them he stopped. He nudged it with his nose.

Kirchgasse. Their street. Their building.

There was a circle there. Beside it were the words *Lina V. – 1974.*

The circle was crossed out. Two lines, in an X.

“She’s free,” Theo said quietly. “Mrs. Silber crossed her out.”

“Mrs. Silber has been missing for months,” Nora said.

She leaned closer over the paper. The lines were shiny. Just a little. The way ink shines before it dries.

“Theo. Touch it.”

Theo touched the X with his finger. He pulled it back. His fingertip was black.

“It’s fresh,” he whispered. “It’s totally fresh.”

Nora’s stomach turned into a cold lump. Ink dries fast. This X couldn’t be more than a few minutes old.

But the map had been behind the loose board until just a little while ago. And since then, Nora hadn’t let go of it.

“Somebody was here,” Theo said. “Somebody came in here and did that.”

He turned around and stared down the hallway. Empty. The apartment door was open, the way they had left it. The stairwell behind it was silent.

Nora looked over at Shadow. He would have known. He always knew when someone was there.

Shadow sniffed the black X. For a long time. Then he sat down and looked at Nora. Calm. No growling. No raised hackles. His tail tapped the floor once.

As if he had recognized something.

That was almost worse than growling.

Nora looked at the board under the sink. At the dust in front of it. A thick gray layer. Only two sets of marks in it. One from Shadow’s nose. And her own fingerprints from earlier.

Nothing else. Nobody had opened the hiding place. Not for months.

And yet the ink was wet.

# Chapter 15: The Cemetery

Nora spread the map out on her bedroom floor. She weighed down the corners with books. Theo knelt next to her. His fingertip was still gray, even though he had washed it three times.

“I can’t get it off,” he said. “Now I’ve got a ghost finger.”

“It’s ink.”

“Ghost ink. On my finger. Forever.”

Shadow was lying by the door. His eyes were half closed. But every time Nora moved the map, his ears twitched.

Nora leaned over the circles. Next to some of the names were small notes. Pencil, scribbled in a hurry.

At the cemetery: *Heinrich B. – 1887.* Below it, two words. *Angry. Grave.*

“Angry. Grave. The end,” Theo read. “Oh, great. That sounds cozy.”

In the city park: *Clara B. – 1889. Child. Lost.* Theo’s grin disappeared.

At a school: *Helene D. – 1921. Veil thin.*

Theo’s head snapped up. “Please tell me there’s no ghost at my school.”

“The elementary school on the Ring. Not yours.”

“Yet.”

Nora ran her finger over the other circles. Factory. Church. Bridge. City hall. Behind each one, a name. Behind each one, someone who couldn’t leave.

“These aren’t monsters,” Theo said. He sounded surprised. “These are people. Normal people with normal names.”

“Yeah.” Nora thought of Lina. Of the round letters and the purple ink.

Her finger went back to the cemetery. It was only a few blocks away, across from the library. They had walked past it yesterday. Shadow had stared at it and growled.

“We’ll go look at it,” Nora said. “Just from the outside. Just to look.”

Theo stared at her. “Angry. Grave. The end. And you want to look.”

“From the other side of the street.”

“Like at the zoo,” Theo muttered. “Except at the zoo there are bars between me and the animals.”

“There’s a fence.”

“Ghosts go through walls, Nora.”

But he got up and grabbed the leash.

---

Long bands of shade stretched across the sidewalk in the low evening sun. The air smelled like warm stone. Shadow trotted between them. Calm. His fur flat.

Until they reached the library.

Shadow stopped. Mid-step. As if he had turned to stone.

“Shadow?” Theo tugged on the leash. “Come on.”

Shadow didn’t move. He stared across the street. At the old cemetery.

Then the city got quieter. The hum of the traffic. The voices at the intersection. As if someone had turned down the volume. A metallic taste settled on Nora’s tongue. Right there in the warm evening sun, the air turned cold.

The dog’s hackles rose. From his neck all the way to his tail. A growl came from deep in his chest. Nora could feel it in her bones.

“Theo,” she whispered.

“I see it,” Theo whispered back. “I mean, I don’t see anything. But I see that he sees something.”

The cemetery lay in the shade of old linden trees. Crooked gravestones, green with moss. A rusty iron gate, half open. Behind it, a gravel path that disappeared between the graves.

Nora let out her breath. A small white cloud hung in front of her mouth.

In July. In the evening sun.

On the fence across the street, something glittered. Nora squinted. A fine white coating covered the iron bars. Frost. It crept up the bars, bit by bit. As if the cold were growing out of the cemetery.

“Nora,” Theo said very quietly. “The fence is freezing.”

“I know.”

“Fences don’t freeze in summer.”

“I know, Theo.”

Then she saw him.

Someone was standing between the gravestones.

Nora’s heart pounded in her ears.

A figure. Gray. See-through. She could make out the gravestone behind the figure. The outline of a man, blurry like smoke.

He stood still. And stared.

Straight at them.

Theo held the leash with both hands. His knuckles were white. Shadow was shaking all over.

The figure lifted his head. Slowly. As if he had all the time in the world.

Then the gate slammed shut.

Iron on iron. A clang that rang down the whole street. No wind. Nobody there. The gate had slammed shut on its own.

Nora flinched so hard she almost tripped.

When she looked again, the figure was gone. Nothing but the gravestones. And the silence.

“Nora.” Theo’s voice was thin as paper. “Was that—”

“Yeah,” Nora whispered. “That was one of them.”

Heinrich B. Angry. Grave.

He had seen them.

---

They ran.

Their feet pounded the sidewalk. Next to her, Theo was panting. Shadow galloped between them, the leash dragging over the cobblestones.

Kirchgasse. Number 28. 22. 18. 14.

Nora yanked the front door open. Stairwell. Warm air. The smell of dinner from the downstairs apartment. Normal. Safe.

Inside. Door shut. Deadbolt locked.

Nora leaned against the wall. Her lungs burned. Theo dropped onto the floor next to her. His face was white.

“He was staring at us,” Theo said. His voice was shaking.

“I know.”

“He wasn’t like Lina.”

“No,” Nora said. “Lina was sad. That one was angry.”

Shadow lay down between them in the hallway. His hackles were still up a little. He was watching the door, as if someone might follow them.

Theo took a deep breath. He sat up straight. He cleared his throat.

“Okay,” he said. “I know what’s coming. Any second now you’re going to say we quit. So listen to me first. Because I prepared a speech.”

Nora looked at him. “We keep going,” she said.

Theo blinked. “What?”

“We keep going.”

“Hold on.” He raised his hand. “I had three points. With examples.”

“Give your speech anyway.”

“Point one: We’re good at this. Point two: Shadow is on our side.” He frowned. “I forgot point three. You totally threw me off.”

Nora couldn’t help laughing. It just came out, right through her pounding heart.

She was scared. Her hands were still shaking. But she thought of Lina. Of *Nobody believes me*. Of the golden light that had gotten smaller and disappeared.

They had done that. She and Theo. And this time Theo didn’t have to talk her into it.

“But only together,” she said. “Nobody goes anywhere alone.”

“Deal.”

“And Shadow always comes with us.”

Shadow lifted his head. But he didn’t look at Nora. He looked at the apartment door.

Then he stood up. He padded to the door and scratched at it once. He looked back. Waiting.

“Not upstairs again,” Theo groaned. “We already went up there today. We went to a cemetery. I’ve got a ghost finger.”

Shadow scratched again.

---

On the third floor, Shadow trotted past the kitchen. Past the room with the list of names. He stopped in the middle of the hallway and lowered his nose to the floor.

Then he lay down. Right on top of one floorboard. He put his paw on it and looked at Nora.

Nora knelt down. The floorboard had a thin gap around it, barely visible. It wasn’t nailed down.

Without a word, Theo handed her the screwdriver. Nora pried. Dust trickled down. The floorboard came up.

Underneath was an envelope. Big and yellowed. Closed with red sealing wax.

There was writing on the front. Mrs. Silber’s scrawly handwriting.

*For those who come after me.*

Below it, smaller, in a shaky hand, a second sentence.

*If you are reading this, I am no longer here.*

# Chapter 16: Those Who Come After Me

They sat down on the hallway floor. Backs against the wall. Shadow lay down between them, his head on Theo’s knee.

“She knew somebody would come,” Theo whispered.

Nora ran her fingers over the writing. *For those who come after me.*

“No,” she said quietly. “She hoped so.”

She broke the seal. The wax cracked into pieces. Inside were several sheets of paper, covered in close, tight writing.

“Hurry up,” Theo said. No joke this time.

Nora began to read. “‘To those who find this letter. My name is Margret Silber. For forty years, I have cared for the Restless of Gravenstedt.’”

She kept reading. Her voice was steady.

“‘The Restless are trapped. About two hundred years ago, someone opened something that was meant to stay closed. A door between the living and the dead. Since then, the dead cannot leave Gravenstedt.’”

Theo stared at her. “A door? Who would do something like that?”

Nora didn’t answer. She thought of the chronicle. *In 1823.* And of the red circle at the edge of the map.

“Trapped,” Theo said quietly. “Like Lina. For fifty years.”

“Some of them much longer.” Nora thought of the years on the map. 1843. 1889. “The one at the cemetery has been standing there since 1887.”

Theo pulled his knees up to his chest. “I don’t want to be stuck somewhere that long. I can’t even stand sitting in the dentist’s waiting room.”

She kept reading. “‘The first person I helped was a girl. Anna. She only wanted someone to say her name out loud. I sat with her for three nights. On the third night, she said thank you. Then she was gone.’”

“Thank you,” Theo repeated quietly. “Like Lina. At the window.”

Nora nodded. Her throat was tight.

“‘I cried for a long time afterward,’” she read. “‘From sadness and from happiness. It is still the same for me with every one of them.’”

---

Nora turned the page. “‘Shadow has always led me to them. He senses them before I even suspect it. He is not an ordinary dog.’”

Shadow lifted his head. As if he had recognized his name. His eyes rested on Nora. Calm. Old. Much older than a dog should look.

“‘I do not know who you are. I do not know how old you are. But Shadow found you. That means you are the right ones.’”

Theo quickly wiped his eyes with his sleeve.

“If you cry now,” Nora said, “I’ll cry too.”

“I’m not crying. Dust.”

“Sure. Dust.”

Theo sniffled. “Lots of dust up here. Keep reading.”

“‘The Veil is getting thinner,’” Nora read. “‘It is the border between their world and ours. In some places it is tearing. More of the Restless are coming through it than I can handle alone. There are nights when I hear all of them at once. Then Gravenstedt is loud with voices that no one else hears.’”

Theo’s leg was bouncing. He did that when he was scared and didn’t want to show it.

“‘Please do not give up,’” Nora read. “‘They need someone who listens. Shadow will lead you. Trust him.’”

Nora thought the letter was over. But under the last paragraph there was one more line. The handwriting was different. Smaller. Shakier. As if Mrs. Silber had added it later.

“‘And beware of the First One. He is not like the others.’”

Shadow jumped up.

He didn’t run to the door. He planted himself in front of Nora and Theo. Legs wide apart. Shoulders forward. Like a shield between them and the rest of the apartment.

Theo’s hand hung in the air where Shadow’s ear had been a second ago.

“The First One,” he whispered. “Like in her notebook.”

“She wrote about him in the notebook,” Nora said slowly. “Here she’s writing to us. She’s warning us.”

Shadow growled. Deep. He stared at the hallway window. Out over the rooftops.

Somewhere in Gravenstedt, a church clock struck. Once. Twice.

“Can we go downstairs now?” Theo whispered. “Please?”

---

Nora couldn’t sleep.

She sat by the window. Behind her, the apartment was dark. The radiator ticked. Familiar sounds.

Shadow lay next to her on the floor. His warm body pressed against her leg. His eyes were open. Two bright points in the dim room.

The map was on her desk. Next to it, the letter.

Outside, Gravenstedt spread out in the dark. Rooftops and chimneys. Streetlights that cast yellow circles on the cobblestones. The church tower, black against the dark blue sky.

A completely normal city. At least that’s how it looked.

Nora thought of the circles on the map. Behind lit windows. Under normal roofs. Among people who had no idea. Eleven who were still waiting.

A few days ago, she would have laughed if someone had talked about ghosts. Now she sat at the window at night and listened for sounds no machine could record.

Footsteps came from the hallway. Mom was standing in the doorway. Bathrobe, sleepy eyes.

“Nora? Why are you still up?”

“Couldn’t sleep.”

Mom sat down on the edge of the bed. She brushed a strand of hair out of Nora’s face. “You settled in so fast. Both of you. I’m proud of you.”

*If you only knew*, Nora thought.

“Thanks, Mom.”

At the door, Mom stopped. She looked at Shadow. He lay still and looked back.

“He’s a good dog,” she said. “I’m glad you found him.”

*He found us*, Nora thought. *He sat outside the door and waited.*

“Me too,” she said.

Mom left. A little later, Theo padded in. Messy hair. One eye open.

“Can’t sleep,” he murmured. He sat down next to her on the floor. Shadow immediately laid his head on Theo’s knee.

“Are you scared?” Theo asked.

“Yeah.”

“Me too.” He was quiet for a moment. “You know what I was thinking earlier? When that man was standing there?”

“That you wanted to run?”

“Nope. I was thinking, he’s been standing there since 1887. Alone. And nobody comes.” He pulled his knees up. “That’s worse than creepy. That’s sad.”

Nora looked at him. Sometimes she forgot that he was only ten.

“Theo?”

“Hmm?”

“You’re the bravest ten-year-old I know.”

“I’m the only ten-year-old you know.”

“Still.”

He grinned and yawned at the same time. “I’m going back to bed. One of us has to get enough sleep.” He shuffled out.

A little later, a murmur came from his room. “… need … bigger flashlight …” Nora smiled.

---

She got up and went to the desk. She went to roll up the map.

Shadow lifted his head.

Nora put her hand on the paper. And stopped.

Under her fingers, the map was cold.

Not cool. Ice-cold. Only in one spot. Only on a single circle.

At the cemetery.

Fine white lines crept over the ink. Like frost on a windowpane. In the middle of July. In the middle of her room.

*Heinrich B. – 1887.*

Shadow stood next to her. He didn’t growl. He whined. Very softly.

Nora didn’t pull her hand away. The cold bit into her fingertips.

Right then it felt as if another hand were lying flat against the other side.

In exactly the same spot.
