# Chapter 7: Lina

Silence.

The hum of the fluorescent tube above them. The soft clicking of the microfiche machine.

“Nora.” Theo’s voice shook. “A girl lived in our building. Fifty years ago. And died. The same age as you.”

Nora’s throat tightened. Cold spread through her chest, a cold that had nothing to do with the library’s air-conditioning. Twelve years old. Kirchgasse 14. She could picture the girl. In the kitchen where Nora ate cereal every morning. In the hallway Nora walked through every day. In the room where Nora slept.

“The knocking,” Theo said quietly. “The cold. The word on the window.” He broke off. He didn’t have to say the rest.

“We don’t know that,” Nora said. But there was no fight left in her voice. The words sounded hollow.

“Yes, we do.” Theo looked at her. No jokes. No sarcasm. His eyes were dark and serious. “She lived there. And she’s still there.”

Nora stared at the screen. Six lines. A whole life, summed up in six lines. And then forgotten. For fifty years.

Something heavy settled on Nora’s shoulders. A girl had lived in her building. And maybe nobody had listened to her. She couldn’t shake the thought, even though she didn’t know why yet.

“We have to go home,” she said.

“Home,” Theo repeated. “You mean the apartment where a dead girl—”

“Theo.”

He swallowed. “Okay. Yeah. Home.”

---

Outside, Shadow was lying on the warm sidewalk in front of the library. When he saw them, he jumped up. His tail gave a short wag, then stopped. He nudged Nora’s hand and whined softly.

“What’s wrong with him?” Theo said.

“No idea.” Nora knelt down and stroked Shadow’s head. The dog pressed his nose against her arm. He looked up at her, alert and watching.

“Maybe he can feel it,” Theo murmured. “That we found out something.”

“Dogs can’t sense newspaper articles.”

“This dog can sense ghosts. Newspaper articles are the smaller miracle.”

Then Shadow went stiff under her hand. His ears pricked up. He turned his head and looked across the street.

Toward the cemetery.

Old walls. Ivy. Gravestones behind a wrought-iron fence. The cemetery was right across from the library.

Shadow stared at it. Motionless. His hackles rose.

“Shadow?” Theo said quietly.

The dog growled. Low. Deep. Then he shook himself, as if he wanted to throw something off. His fur lay flat again. He looked away from the cemetery, back at Nora.

Nora shivered. Just for a second. Like an ice cube on the back of her neck.

She untied Shadow from the bike rack.

“Let’s go home,” she said.

“Best idea all day,” Theo said.

They set off. Nora took one last look at the cemetery. Between the gravestones—had something moved? Gray. Fast. Then it was gone.

She walked faster. But she could still feel the cemetery. On her left, behind the wall. Like something waiting.

Shadow trotted along beside her. Close. Closer than usual. His fur brushed her leg with every step.

She stopped in front of the building. Gray walls. Narrow windows. She had looked at this building again and again since yesterday. But now she saw it differently. Now she knew who had died here.

“Nora?” Theo said.

“Yeah.”

“Are you going to stand here till it gets dark, or are we going in?”

She went in. Up the stairs. Opened the apartment door.

Mom was standing in the hallway, sorting the mail. She looked up. “You two look worn out. Where were you?”

“The library,” Nora said.

Mom nodded. Then, casually: “The neighbor told me today that the old woman upstairs is still missing. Silber, I think her name was. Gone for months. Weird, huh?”

Nora could feel Theo’s eyes on her back. She swallowed. “Does the neighbor know her?”

Mom shook her head. “Barely. Apparently she was always alone. With a dog.” She added a letter to the pile. “Kind of strange, right?”

Then she yawned. “I’ve been on my feet since five. I’m going to bed early tonight. There’s food in the fridge.”

The bedroom door clicked shut behind her.

Shadow went ahead. Down the hallway, into the living room, into the kitchen. He sniffed the corners. His fur lay flat.

Nora went into the kitchen. Automatically. She wanted a glass of water. Her throat was as dry as paper.

Then she saw the window.

Condensation. Again. The window was fogged up. In the afternoon. In summer. Impossible. But there it was.

New letters. Two words this time. Clear and sharp. As if written with a child’s finger.

DON’T FORGET

Nora’s glass slipped out of her hand. It crashed onto the tiles and broke into three pieces. She barely noticed. She stood in front of the window and stared at the words.

Don’t forget.

Her breath came way too fast. Her arms went heavy, as if someone had hung weights on them.

“Theo,” she said, her voice rough. “Come here.”

He came. Saw the window. This time he didn’t go pale. This time he just nodded. Slowly. As if he had expected it.

“She’s talking to us,” he said quietly. “Lina is talking to us.”

Don’t forget. Nora didn’t touch the window. This time she didn’t wipe anything away. She just stood there and read the words. Over and over.

Don’t forget. As if Lina had waited fifty years for someone to find her story. And now that they had, she was begging: Don’t forget me. Not again. Not one more time.

Nora’s eyes burned. She blinked. Once. Twice.

“We won’t forget you,” she whispered.

The words stayed on the window. Silent. Still. Like an echo from long ago.

Theo came and stood next to her. His arm touched hers. He didn’t say anything. He just stood there. Sometimes that was enough.

“We’ll find out what happened,” Nora said. Her voice was quiet, but steady. “We’ll find out.”

---

Then they heard it. From the living room.

A scratching sound. Soft. Steady. Claws on plaster.

Nora turned around. Theo followed her.

Shadow was sitting in the living room. Right in the middle of the long wall. In front of the old flowered wallpaper. He sat upright. His back was straight. His ears pointed forward.

He was scratching at the wall with his right paw. Slowly. Always in the same spot. Not frantic. Not nervous. Calm and determined. As if he knew exactly what he was doing.

“Shadow?” Theo said.

The dog didn’t react. He kept scratching. His claws left thin lines in the old wallpaper.

“What’s he doing?” Theo whispered.

Nora went closer. Shadow lifted his head for a moment. His eyes met hers. Then he looked down again and kept scratching. In the same spot. Over and over.

In exactly the same spot.

A tingle ran over Nora’s skin. She put her hand on the wall, right next to Shadow’s paw. The wallpaper was old and rough under her fingers. Underneath, she could feel something. A crack. A bump that didn’t belong there.

“There’s something behind the wall,” Nora said.

Theo took a step back. “In movies, that’s usually the line right before everybody screams.”

Shadow kept scratching. Patiently. Without stopping. In exactly the same spot.

As if he wanted to show them where they had to look.
