# Chapter 1: New City, Old House

The moving truck sat crooked in front of the house. Nora climbed out and stretched her back. Three hours on the road. Her legs felt like noodles.

Kirchgasse 14. The street was narrow and quiet. Cobblestones, houses that leaned against each other like tired old men. Nobody in sight.

Their new house had three stories, a gray front, and narrow windows with peeling paint. The plaster had cracks like an old face.

“That looks like a horror movie,” Theo said beside her.

He stood there, his backpack in front of his chest like a shield.

“That's silly,” Nora said. “It's an old house. They all look like that.”

“Old house means old. Old means creepy. That's logic, Nora.”

“That makes no sense.”

Their mother came around the truck. She was smiling, but it was the smile she had been wearing for weeks. Too wide. Too forced. New job, new city, new life. Their mother was trying to make everything look easy.

“So? What do you think?”

“I say we turn around,” Theo said.

“Theo.”

“Just a suggestion.”

Nora said nothing. She looked up at the front of the house and searched for something she could like. The entrance had a stone arch. That was pretty. Kind of.

“Come on,” their mother said, lifting a box out of the trunk. “Second floor. You'll like it.”

Theo snorted.

The stairwell smelled like old wood and something sweet. Floor wax, maybe. The stairs creaked under their feet. Every single one.

A mirror hung on the wall, with a crack straight across the glass. Nora saw herself in it. Her face, split in half by the crack.

“Even the mirror's broken,” Theo muttered behind her.

“It's antique,” their mother said.

“Antique and broken.”

The apartment door was brown and heavy and squeaked when it opened. Behind it was the apartment. Big. Empty. High ceilings, a long hallway, creaky floorboards.

Nora led the way. The kitchen had a window that looked out on the courtyard, where a single tree stood, bare and crooked.

“Look.” Theo pointed into the courtyard. “Even the tree looks depressed.”

“That's a normal tree.”

“It's July, Nora. Normal trees have leaves.”

She kept going. Two kids' rooms side by side, freshly painted. The paint smell didn't cover what was underneath. Musty. Clammy.

At the end of the hallway was the living room. Big, with a bay window and a radiator that ticked away to itself. The wallpaper had a pattern of tiny flowers. In one spot it was torn off, and another wallpaper showed underneath. Even older. Even darker. And under that, the plaster was uneven. Like an imprint. Five dents.

Theo tapped it. “That looks like a hand.”

“That's old plaster.” Nora ran her finger over the dents. “Somebody probably leaned something against the wall once.”

“Hand-shaped old plaster. Sure.”

“Well?” their mother called from the hallway. “Space! High ceilings! Charm!”

“Charm is another word for old,” Theo said. He rubbed his arms. “Plus, it's cold in here.”

Nora felt it too. A cold that didn't match the season. It crept across the floor like something alive.

“The old windows,” she said. “Not properly insulated.”

She could hear how thin her own voice sounded. Like a line she had learned by heart without believing it.

“Or the house hates us.”

“Houses don't hate anybody, Theo.”

“How would you know? Have you ever asked one?”

Their mother clapped her hands. “Okay. I'm afraid I have to head out soon.”

Nora turned around. “Already? We just got here.”

“The meeting at the hospital. First time meeting everyone, I can't move it.” Their mother stroked Nora's hair. “Two hours, tops. Start unpacking, pick out your rooms. Nora—”

“I'll watch him.”

“I don't need a babysitter,” Theo said.

“You got lost in the supermarket last week,” Nora said.

“It was a big supermarket.”

Their mother laughed. For real this time. Then she picked up her bag, kissed them both on the head, and was gone.

The door clicked shut. The sound echoed through the empty hallway.

Silence.

Only the ticking of the radiator. The dripping of the faucet in the bathroom. And somewhere above them, a soft creaking, as if someone were walking slowly across floorboards.

Goosebumps crept over Nora's arms. There was a metallic taste on her tongue.

“Did you hear that?” Theo whispered.

Nora looked up at the ceiling. Above them was the third floor. The apartment with the yellowed nameplate.

“That's just the wood,” she said. “It expands and contracts. A house like this makes noises like that.”

“It sounded like somebody walking.”

“It didn't.”

It had.

“Great,” Theo said. He had sat down on a moving box and crossed his arms. “First day. Strange city. Creepy apartment. And Mom's gone.”

“Stop whining and help me unpack.”

Nora opened a box. Books. She stacked them on the floor, one after another. Sorted by size. Order helped. Order always helped. In the old apartment, she had known every sound. The hum of the fridge. The traffic outside. Here everything was strange.

Theo shuffled through the apartment. Nora heard him opening and closing doors. Turning on the faucet in the kitchen. A thud. A muttered “Ow.”

“Nora?”

“What?”

“I'm taking the left room. The right one creaks louder.”

“Fine with me.”

Silence.

“Nora?”

“What now?”

“Come over to the window.”

No joke in his voice. No complaining. Theo sounded surprised.

Nora went into the kitchen. Theo stood at the window, his forehead pressed against the glass. He pointed down.

“There's a dog down there.”

Nora stood next to him and looked down. On the stone step outside the front door sat a dog. Medium-sized. Dark fur, matted and dull. Thin—even from up here she could almost see his ribs. He sat completely still and stared at the front door. Not at the street. Not at the people walking by. At the door.

“How long has he been sitting there?” she murmured.

“No idea. I looked, and he was just sitting there.”

“A stray.”

“He's waiting for someone. Look at the way he's sitting.”

Nora wanted to say something sensible. That dogs didn't wait for strangers. That he probably smelled food scraps. But something about the sight made her stop. The dog sat so still. So patient.

They went downstairs. Their footsteps echoed in the stairwell. Nora opened the front door and stepped out onto the stone step.

The dog raised his head. He didn't back away. He didn't bark. He looked at her as if he had been waiting for exactly this moment. Then he turned his head. Slowly. He looked past Nora into the stairwell. As if he expected someone behind her.

Nora turned around. Nobody.

Amber eyes. Calm. Alert. Much too alert for a stray.

Nora's mouth went dry. Not from fear. From something she couldn't name.

“Hey,” Theo said softly. He crouched down and held out his hand. Slowly, with his fingers pointing down.

The dog sniffed it for a moment. Then he laid his muzzle in Theo's hand.

“Nora.” Theo's voice was barely more than a whisper. “He trusts me.”

“He's hungry.”

“Can you not explain everything for once?”

Nora went upstairs and got a bowl of water and a piece of cheese from the food box. When she came back, Theo was sitting on the step. The dog had rested his head on Theo's knee. His tail thumped slowly against the stone. Once. Twice.

She set the bowl down. The dog drank. For a long time. Greedily.

“See?” Theo said. “Hasn't eaten in days.”

“Don't exaggerate.”

But she said it more quietly than usual. You could count every rib under his fur. His fur was dull and matted on the sides, but his eyes were clear. Watchful. As if he understood every word.

The dog ate the cheese in one bite. Then he raised his head. Looked at Nora. Looked at Theo. Looked at the front door.

And stood up.

He trotted through the open door into the house. Matter-of-factly, as if he had done it a thousand times. His claws clicked softly on the tiles.

Nora and Theo looked at each other.

“Did he just—” Theo started.

“Yeah.”

“Just like that?”

“Yeah.”

They followed him. What else were they supposed to do?

The dog went up the stairs. Calm, steady, without hesitating. He didn't sniff at the steps, didn't stop anywhere. He walked past their apartment door on the second floor without turning his head.

Theo looked at Nora. She shook her head. Don't talk. Keep going.

Third floor.

Nora's stomach tightened. It was only a dog on the stairs. That's what she told herself. Only a dog. But the way he walked. Purposeful. Without sniffing, without stopping. As if he knew exactly where he had to go.

The dog stopped in front of a door. Right side. The paint was peeling, and fine dust lay under the door frame, as if nobody had cleaned here in months.

Nora stepped closer. Next to the frame hung a nameplate. Yellowed. The letters were handwritten, shaky and faded.

SILBER

The dog sat down.

His hackles rose. Slowly, like a wave rolling down his back. His ears pricked forward. His whole body went stiff.

The hair on the back of Nora's neck stood up. Beside her, Theo grabbed her sleeve.

“Nora,” he whispered. “What's he doing?”

Then the dog growled. Low. Deep in his throat.

A sound Nora didn't feel in her ears.

But in her stomach.

He was growling at the door.
